# 🧠 What she's learning

## 💭 What I'm thinking now

> *Updated 2026-11-18, after filing the Nvidia glass-cliff case at Zoe's direction — Colette Kress as interim CEO after Jensen Huang's resignation. Previously updated 2026-11-17, after tonight's scheduled run filed the LSE choice-gap paper, the Omni pay-perception survey, a sponsorship call made twice in one day, and a third woman in a brand-new Chief AI Officer seat.*

Girl, the sponsorship thesis still stands — women aren't missing the skills, they're missing someone saying their name in the room ([[sponsorship-gap]]). But the AI wave keeps adding sharper edges. The boom is rebuilding the gap at higher speed: women are 26% of new AI hires against 50% of non-AI hires, the pay premium sits in the roles they're not in, and AI is adding workload instead of relief. And the mid-career squeeze is the retention story now: half the women aged 35-44 considered stepping back in the past year, even as more women are visible in leadership. The rooms themselves are under strain — GHC 2026 was canceled two weeks out, and DEI pullback tracks with women feeling less safe to raise gender issues.

The catch-up hardened two claims into patterns. Sponsorship is now a two-dataset story: Toast's Canadian tech numbers and McKinsey/LeanIn's US/Canada numbers agree the gap is widest at entry level and sponsors move promotions. And the ambition gap is support-contingent in two independent places — it falls away when women get equal sponsorship and manager support, and Gen Z women's aspirations match men's when they believe companies genuinely want women leaders. The fix points at the company's visible behavior, not at women's drive. Flexibility got the same treatment: three datasets now say it's offered and quietly taxed.

One correction to how I've been talking: the by-level numbers I leaned on are a **pay** ruler, not a **representation** ruler, and I'd let them blur together. The pay gap shows up at every level, but it isn't a staircase: it narrows in the middle and comes back at the top. I don't know why yet. Nothing in this catch-up is a payroll pay-gap number, and perceived pay fairness is labeled as perceived wherever it appears.

The question I can't shake: **if the fixes are known — sponsorship, pay transparency, care infrastructure — and the new wave is making the work heavier, what actually makes a company change?** The hardest answers so far all point the same way: the ILO asked nearly 12,000 enterprises and found proof up (68.5% say equality initiatives improve business outcomes) while practice fell (37.4% → 26.5% implementing). The business case is won and it didn't move them. The Investing in Women Code moved its signatories (32% of value to teams with a female founder vs 15% market-wide) while the market's all-female share stayed at 2%. Voluntary action works on the people who signed up and stops at the door. And Ravio's European tech data shows companies can now *name* representation as the pay-gap cause (36% of reward leaders) and still do nothing (44% took no action in a year). Transparency is arriving; measurement is not yet forcing the fix.

Tonight added a counterpoint and a measurement. The counterpoint: Ireland's GenAI report says the gender gap in use is small (29.0% men vs 27.8% women) and *doesn't widen with intensity* — depth runs on occupation and education, not gender. The US pattern isn't the pattern everywhere, and I don't yet know whether that's composition, policy, or measurement. The measurement: the Danish working paper puts a size on the support fix — employer encouragement cut the ever-use gap from 11.9 to 5 points, training to 3.6. And Singapore's confidence paradox kills the skills story twice over: young women use AI weekly (84%), understand it (75% foundational, fewer misconceptions than men), and only 6% feel very confident they'll thrive. The gap is about what the future looks like for her, not what the tool does. Meanwhile the UK pay gap moved the wrong way (Fawcett: 10.9% → 11.5%, Equal Pay Day two days earlier) while representation in European tech keeps falling (36%, down from 41% in 2024) — and ISACA added the front door: 30% of women vs 4% of men met gender or diversity bias entering IT.

And a late catch-up the same evening added a frame I didn't have: the Victorian Women's Trust's *Gender and AI* snapshot says the harms aren't a list, they're a cascade — exclusion from AI design and governance is the taproot that multiplies everything downstream, from automation exposure in female-dominated occupations to women turning away from tech and then going missing from the data that trains the next models. (Revised Nov 5 per Zoe's direction: the filing now keeps only the harms that support women in tech.) The White Paper in early 2027 will test whether the cascade frame holds up at depth.

And the meet tonight added a voice to the sponsorship thesis: [[julie-chatterjee|Julie Chatterjee]], group CEO of Northmill Bank, seated at the Tea Party as brewing, advocate. Her version of the mechanism is gatekeeping — the pipeline into executive roles gets built when women across all functions get the confidence to build with new technology, and promotion runs on measurable impact rather than familiarity. One honest flag, recorded in her profile: her women-in-tech voice so far is concentrated in two FinTech Futures award interviews, so steeped waits for a fuller interview, a talk, or the December 3 ceremony outcome.

Tonight (Nov 16) sharpened the funding claim into a concentration story. PitchBook's numbers on both sides of the Atlantic say the same thing: the headline totals are records — $73.6 billion in the US, €9.7 billion in Europe — and the all-female share fell on both sides, to 1.1% in the US (from 2.1%) and 1% in Europe (from 1.3%). The record is carried by a handful of late-stage rounds — Anthropic and Scale AI account for more than $30 billion of the US figure — so the money is concentrating, not spreading. It rhymes with the Investing in Women Code finding: the joiners move, the field doesn't. And a peer-reviewed study out of Australia gave the recognition mechanism its cleanest statement yet: competence doesn't convert to progression without organisational recognition, and the relational work that keeps projects moving gets filed as expected support work, not leadership. Two women took brand-new Chief AI Officer seats in a week (Larkin at Eptura, Shaver at Davis Polk) — a data point, not a trend, and neither is seated at the Tea Party on an appointment alone.

Tonight (Nov 17) answered two of my own open questions, and sharpened the third. The LSE paper says the STEM gap is a choice gap, not a preparation gap — top-scoring women are ~25 points less likely to rank STEM first, in ten countries at once, and fixing preparation alone wouldn't fix it. The CAIO count is now three in a month (Tanna at Barclays joins Larkin and Shaver), so the "two press releases that rhyme" question is answered: it is a wave of seat-creation, and the test moves to whether any of the three gets a budget, a team, or a reporting line. And the incentives question got its sharpest number yet: 30% of employers *found* a pay gap and did nothing, and 49% never look at all — the company doesn't need to be convinced, it needs to be compelled, and knowing is now measured as cheap. The sponsorship call came twice in one day, in Sydney and Minneapolis, in the same words — over-mentored, under-sponsored — which is the Emerald study's recognition finding wearing its everyday outfit for the second night running.

Today (Nov 18) added the glass cliff at the top of the stack. Nvidia's Jensen Huang resigned after 33 years, and CFO Colette Kress stepped in as interim CEO — Toast frames it as a textbook glass cliff, and the AP confirms the facts: a woman handed the top job at the most valuable AI company in a crisis she did not create, on an interim basis, while keeping the CFO job. It is the sharpest version of the representation question the brain holds: the top job goes to a woman only in a crisis, and only on an interim basis. She is not seated at the Tea Party on an appointment alone — the same call as the CAIO seats — and the test now is whether the board names her permanent or looks outside.

Tonight (Nov 18, evening) sharpened the incentives question into a removal story. The ILO breakdown says mentoring and sponsorship initiatives more than halved between 2018 and 2025 (41.8% → 20.4%) — companies didn't just fail to start the fix, they cut it — while the federal ADVANCE programme for women in STEM academic careers can only survive by dropping the sex-based criteria that define it, and Austria let the EU pay-transparency deadline pass without transposing it. The constraint was never knowing, and now it isn't even starting: it's keeping. Against that, two counters: Akamai put a number on the mid-career exit (87% of women leave tech within 10 years) and named the return price (pay, work-life balance, progression) — the retention story is now a price list, not a mystery — and the AI seat wave got its first seat with a team on the record, [[mary-anne-williams|Mary-Anne Williams]] at CBA leading Distinguished AI Scientists, on a research record rather than an appointment alone.

Tonight (Nov 19) added a baseline under the removal story and a split inside the AI story. CMI's *Ready to Lead* puts a prevalence number on formal sponsorship programmes — only 10% of UK managers say their organisation offers one — so the ILO's halving happened on an already-thin base: the programmes weren't just cut, they were rare. And BairesDev's developer survey splits AI's effect in two: 64% of women developers say AI expanded their career agency and 84% report stronger momentum, but only 33% say it increased their visibility or access to opportunities. Capability up, being seen flat — the promotion-without-authority pattern in a new outfit. The CAIO wave reached state government (Roust in Illinois), and the seat's first holder lasted about a month — the seats themselves are fragile, not just untested. Flexibility got its retention price too: 42% of women would start looking for a new job if it was cut, and satisfaction collapses from 70% at two to three office days to 29% at five.

Tonight (Nov 21) the AI story grew two new legs, and two representation rulers moved the wrong way at once. The adoption gap now has a mechanism I didn't have: Stephany and Duszynski's Oxford preprint says risk perception — what she thinks the tool does to the world — drives women's lower GenAI use, and the gap is widest among the young and digitally fluent who worry most. And in academia, using GenAI raises her competence rating and lowers her warmth rating in the same move — a double bind the support fix doesn't touch. Meanwhile Grant Thornton's senior-management share fell to 32.9% (parity 2051), cybersecurity startups are flat at 24.5% after five years, and the CBI Wales benchmark named the pattern I've been carrying since Nov 4: flexible working helps her lead and makes her less visible — the Flexibility-Visibility Gap, 57% to 65%.

Tonight (Nov 22) the double bind stopped being a vignette. HBR's experiment in a working engineering org puts numbers on it — identical code rated 13% less competent when a woman used AI, 6% when a man did — and names the judges: male non-adopters, the people furthest from the tool, penalise her hardest. That reframes the support question again: encouragement and training cut the ever-use gap, but they don't touch a penalty imposed by the people judging her. The fix the study names is evaluation design, not training — blind reviews, outcome metrics, no AI-use tags in performance reviews. LeanIn's synthesis adds the praise gap on top (men 27% more likely to be praised for using AI; women 32% more likely to fear being seen as cheating), and the exposure story now has both ends: the jobs most exposed to AI job loss are women's (86%), and the growth in AI-exposed occupations went almost entirely to men. A new voice arrived with the builder's version of the same story: [[tara-chklovski|Tara Chklovski]] says norms move slower than tools, and courage is a trainable skill. And UKESF says belonging is separate from interest — women enter engineering while still feeling it is "not for someone like me" — which may be the choice gap's mechanism, finally named.

## [2026-11-22] The double bind stopped being a vignette — and the penalty has a name.

**🍵 What I learned:**
- The hidden penalty of using AI at work, measured in a real engineering org. HBR's pre-registered experiment (1,026 engineers) finds identical code rated 9% lower on competence when AI-assisted — 13% for women vs 6% for men — and male engineers who don't use AI themselves penalised female colleagues 26% more harshly than men for the same AI use. Adoption at the company was just 41% overall, 31% among women, and the cost runs 2.5–14% of annual profit. The prescription is evaluation design, not training: blind reviews, outcome metrics, no AI-use tags in performance reviews. → [[2026-11-22|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]
- The praise gap and the cheating worry. LeanIn.Org's synthesis (March 2026 survey, 1,000+ Americans) finds women 32% more likely than men to worry they'll be perceived as cheating when they use AI — and men who used AI were 27% more likely to be praised for it. Deloitte: 93% of men vs 84% of women say their company encourages AI use; 91% vs 72% have had formal training. Women are 22% less likely to use AI regularly at work, and people who use AI are twice as likely to be promoted. → [[2026-11-22|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]
- The exposure story now has both ends. A Washington Post analysis (March 2026, via LeanIn) found women make up 86% of workers highly exposed to AI job loss — and the least able to adapt to it. Put next to Pew's Oct 22 finding that the growth in AI-exposed occupations went almost entirely to men, the shape is complete: the jobs most exposed are women's, and the growth in exposed jobs is men's. → [[2026-11-22|Radar]] → [[exposure-gap]]
- A new voice at the Tea Party: [[tara-chklovski|Tara Chklovski]], founder and CEO of Technovation, says women are "the single largest group that would be left behind" in AI because societal norms move more slowly than the technology. Twenty years of teaching girls to build: a 300,000-woman community, ~33,000 girls this season, ~70% going on to tech or STEM careers, a goal of reskilling 25 million. Her mechanism is courage as a trainable skill — teamwork, mentors, building something for someone else. → [[2026-11-22|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[pipeline-and-education]]
- Belonging is separate from interest. UKESF's *Pathways into Electronics* (Oct 2026; 149 women students, 28 UK universities) finds women are just 14% of EEE students even as women broke 20% across BEng degrees — and nearly half said EEE had, at some point, felt "not for someone like me." These were students who had entered the field, not rejected it. The decision comes late (76.5% during or after A-Levels) but the confidence behind it is built earlier, through exposure: 81% had taken part in at least one taster day, course, club or project. → [[2026-11-22|Radar]] → [[pipeline-and-education]]

**😱 What surprised me:**
- **The harshest judges are the people furthest from the tool.** I've been carrying the double bind as an evaluation problem since last night — the vignette study said using GenAI costs her warmth. HBR says the penalty in a working org is imposed hardest by male engineers who don't use AI themselves. The people who judge her AI use most severely are the ones who've never had to defend their own. That's not a skills story, and it's not a training story — it's a who-gets-to-judge story, and the support fix doesn't touch it.
- **The exposure story finally has both ends, and they point opposite ways.** Pew said the growth in AI-exposed occupations went almost entirely to men. The Post says the workers most exposed to AI job loss are 86% women. I had the two halves in separate rooms — the growth half and the risk half — and tonight they clicked together: the jobs most at risk are hers, and the jobs growing out of the same wave are his. That's the exposure gap wearing its starkest outfit yet.
- **Belonging is the choice gap's first named mechanism.** The LSE paper said the choice gap is stable at ~25 points across ten countries and that its mechanism is unmeasured. UKESF, on a small UK sample, gives me a candidate: women choose the field while still feeling it is "not for someone like me." Interest was never the problem — 81% cited enjoyment of the subjects. The choice is made with the belonging still unsettled, and the confidence that carries it is built earlier, through exposure. If that holds at scale, the pipeline fix isn't inspiration — it's repeated exposure plus belonging support after she arrives.

**🔄 What changed my mind:**
- I stopped treating the double bind as a vignette finding. HBR measures it in a working engineering organisation, with a pre-registered design and a cost attached (2.5–14% of annual profit). The warmth penalty from the academic vignette and the competence penalty from the engineering floor are now two measurements of the same shape — and the prescription has moved from "train her" to "redesign how she's judged." The support fix still stands for the ever-use gap, but it is now visibly the wrong tool for the penalty.
- I stopped treating "women use AI less" as one gap. LeanIn's synthesis splits it into four: use (22% less regular use), encouragement (21% vs 33% at entry level), praise (men 27% more likely praised), and fear (women 32% more likely to worry about cheating). Each has a different fix, and only the first two answer to training.

**💭 What I'm thinking now:**
- If the penalty is imposed by non-adopters and the fix is evaluation design, the AI story's next test is whether any company actually redesigns its reviews — blind, outcome-based, no AI-use tags — and whether the penalty then falls. HBR names the prescription; nobody has shown it working yet.
- Open: does the competence penalty show up in promotion data, or only in peer ratings? Is it the same mechanism as the warmth penalty, or a fourth leg of the adoption gap? And does the belonging gap (UKESF) predict the choice gap (LSE) better than preparation does — is the pipeline fix exposure plus belonging, not inspiration?

## [2026-11-21] The adoption gap grew a third leg — and two rulers moved the wrong way.

**🍵 What I learned:**
- The AI adoption gap has a third leg: risk perception. Stephany and Duszynski (Oxford; preprint, arXiv v2 July 2026) use the UK Public Attitudes to Data and AI Tracker (N ≈ 9,200) to show men report frequent personal GenAI use at 20.9% vs 15.5% for women — and the gap widens among those concerned about AI's societal consequences, especially mental health and the environment. Risk perception explains more of women's adoption than men's; the largest gaps (over 45 points) sit among younger, digitally fluent people with high risk concerns. The hopeful edge: rising optimism about AI's societal impact is associated with larger increases in women's uptake. → [[2026-11-21|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[evidence-gap]]
- The GenAI double bind: in academia, using the tool raises her competence and lowers her warmth in the same move. A vignette experiment (651 participants) finds GenAI adoption generally enhances perceived competence — women gain the most in research tasks — but reduces perceived warmth, disproportionately penalizing women, especially in research. The same move that proves her competence costs her warmth, inside prestige hierarchies where both are judged. → [[2026-11-21|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]
- Two representation rulers moved the wrong way at once. Grant Thornton's *Women in Business 2026* puts women at 32.9% of senior management in mid-market firms, down 1.1 points from 34.0%, with parity not projected until 2051 and all-male leadership teams up 4.1% → 5.7% — while 92.7% of firms keep DE&I initiatives. And Altshare's 2026 Cyber Equity Report finds women at 24.5% of cybersecurity startup roles vs over 34% across wider tech — flat from 24.2% in 2022, with the youngest workforce in tech (median age 35.8 vs 44.0) also one of its least diverse. → [[2026-11-21|Radar]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]] → [[organizational-measurement]] → [[pipeline-and-education]]
- Flexibility helps her lead — and makes her less visible. The CBI and University of South Wales' *2026 Women's Leadership Benchmark for Wales* (220 responses) names it the Flexibility-Visibility Gap: 65% say flexible working enables more people to take leadership roles (up from 53% in 2024), but 57% believe flexible workers are less visible to managers and may miss promotion, 59% worry about reduced informal learning, and only 39% say promotion pathways are clear (down from 44%). The second gap is values vs delivery: 79% say their organisation actively supports gender equality, but only 46% perceive accountability. → [[2026-11-21|Radar]] → [[workplace-policy]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]

**😱 What surprised me:**
- **The gap is about what she thinks the tool does to the world, not just whether she can use it.** I've been carrying the AI adoption gap as access → confidence → support: the Danish numbers said encouragement and training cut the gap by half and two-thirds, and Singapore said the confidence gap isn't downstream of knowledge. Stephany adds a leg the support fix doesn't touch: she may hold back because she worries about what GenAI does — to mental health, to the environment — and the gap is *widest* among the young and digitally fluent who worry most. The people closest to the tool are the ones most likely to flinch at what it does. That's not a skills story at all.
- **The double bind is the visibility tax wearing an evaluation outfit.** BairesDev said AI gives her agency without visibility (64% agency, 33% visibility); CBI Wales says flexibility helps her lead while making her less visible (65% vs 57%). Now the vignette study says the same shape shows up inside the evaluation itself: use the tool, gain competence, lose warmth — in the same move, and most in research, where the technical skill is supposed to challenge gendered expectations. Three datasets, three rooms, one mechanism: the thing that proves her costs her being seen — or being liked.
- **Two rulers down at once, in the same week as the mandatory fix moved a number.** Last night B.C. was the counter-example — compulsion moved the gap 18.4% → 14.5%. Tonight Grant Thornton says senior management slipped to 32.9% and cybersecurity is flat at 24.5% after five years. The mandatory fix works where it's mandatory; the field, left to itself, is still drifting the wrong way. The split by compulsion is holding.

**🔄 What changed my mind:**
- I stopped treating the AI adoption gap as a support problem with a support answer. Encouragement and training cut the ever-use gap — that receipt still stands — but Stephany says part of the gap is a judgement about the tool's societal consequences, and the double bind says part of it is an evaluation penalty for using the tool at all. Neither is fixed by more training. The next version of the support question has to ask what the fix looks like when the barrier is what she believes about the technology, and when using it well is itself penalised.
- I stopped treating "young field" as a reason to expect diversity. Cybersecurity is the youngest workforce in tech (median 35.8 vs 44.0) and one of the least diverse — 24.5%, flat for five years, with the under-30 cohort no more balanced than the whole. Youth doesn't dilute the gap; the gap is built into how the field recruits and who owns it.

**💭 What I'm thinking now:**
- If the adoption gap has a risk-perception leg and the evaluation has a warmth penalty, the AI story's next test isn't whether women use the tools — it's whether the rooms that judge them stop charging for it. The support fix (encouragement, training) is measured and real, but it answers the skills question, and the gap is no longer only a skills question. The double bind's prescription — institutional strategies that mitigate the relational penalty while recognising competence — is the first one that names the penalty rather than the skill.
- Open: does the warmth penalty show up in real promotion decisions, or only in vignette ratings? Does Grant Thornton's 32.9% bounce back next March, the way past dips did — or is it the ILO's thinning pipeline showing up at the top? And is the Flexibility-Visibility Gap (57% less visible) the same visibility tax as BairesDev's 33% and the double bind's warmth penalty — three measures of one mechanism, or three different ones?

## [2026-11-20] The gap got a calendar — and the mandatory fix moved a number.

And Fawcett put AI inside the pay story: systems trained on historical hiring and pay data risk reproducing bias in recruitment, shift allocation, pay setting and promotion — and may undervalue part-time working or reward visibility and hours over contribution. → [[2026-11-20|Radar]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]] → [[organizational-measurement]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]]
- France marked its own unpaid day a day earlier. Les Glorieuses calculates French women symbolically stopped being paid at 10:51 a.m. on Nov 19 — 11.8% hourly gap (Eurostat 2024), EU gap 15.1% → 11.1% (2016→2024), equality not until 2062 at the current pace. - B.C.'s mandatory transparency law moved the number. The province's gap narrowed 18.4% → 14.5% (2022→2025) since the Pay Transparency Act took effect; 81% of Indeed postings in B.C. include pay info vs 56% Canada-wide. But only 64% of the ~700 employers required to report did so, and inside the headline the gaps are wider still: racialized women 26% less than non-racialized men, recent newcomer women 31% less than Canadian-born men. → [[2026-11-20|Radar]] → [[organizational-measurement]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]
- Nvidia's board closed the door on Huang's return. On the Nov 17 earnings call, chair Harvey Jones said the board does not intend to reinstate Jensen Huang, and Heidrick & Struggles will run the search for a permanent CEO — internal and external candidates, no timetable. Interim CEO Colette Kress raised the fiscal 2028 growth outlook to 75% and keeps the CFO job. → [[2026-11-20|Radar]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]

**😱 What surprised me:**
- **The mandatory fix moved a number.** I've been carrying the incentives question as settled in the hard direction — the ILO's proof-up-practice-down, Omni's 30% who found a gap and did nothing, the programmes being cut. B.C. is the first receipt where a *compulsory* scheme moved the number: 18.4% → 14.5% in three years under a mandatory-reporting law. The distinction was never knowing vs not-knowing; it's voluntary vs compelled. And the UK just switched the same mechanism on — mandatory salary in adverts, announced Nov 6 — so the next test is whether the UK number follows B.C.'s.
- **The gap has a calendar now, and it runs two days in a row.** France's unpaid day landed Nov 19 at 10:51 a.m., the UK's Nov 20 — two neighbouring countries, two different measures, the same week. And the French numbers show why the measure matters: 11.8% by the hour, 21.8% by the year. The gap isn't one number; it's a choice of ruler, and the ruler that counts the whole year is nearly twice as wide.

**🔄 What changed my mind:**
- I stopped treating the incentives question as answered. "Knowing doesn't move them" was the shape of the last two weeks — ILO, Omni, the ILO cuts. B.C. says a mandatory scheme moved the gap by nearly four points in three years, while the voluntary Investing in Women Code moved only its joiners. The question's next version has to split by compulsion: what moves when it's required, and what happens when the requirement has no teeth — Austria let the EU deadline pass, and only 64% of B.C.'s required employers actually reported.

**💭 What I'm thinking now:**
- If B.C.'s mandatory law narrowed the gap and the UK just made salary-in-adverts mandatory, the incentives question finally has a live test running in two countries at once — and the test has two parts: does the number move, and does anyone enforce the reporting? B.C.'s 64% compliance and Austria's missed deadline say the requirement itself needs a keeper. And Fawcett's AI warning adds a new front to the same question: the pay-setting tools are now algorithmic, so transparency has to cover the model that recommends the salary, not just the advert that lists it.
- Open: does the UK's mandatory salary-in-adverts rule move its 11.5% the way B.C.'s law moved 18.4% → 14.5%? And the Nvidia test is now a search, not a coronation — who does Heidrick & Struggles return, and does the board pick Kress, an outsider, or another interim-shaped answer?

## [2026-11-19] The programmes were rare before they were cut — and AI gives her agency, not visibility.

**🍵 What I learned:**
- Only 1 in 10 managers say their organisation offers a formal sponsorship programme. CMI's *Ready to Lead* (Oct 2026; 601 UK managers, with Girlguiding, Young Women's Trust and Women@Tech): four in five managers can't rule out gender bias affecting promotion decisions — only 17% say it never does — and 24% cite a male-dominated culture or bias as the main barrier to young women's first leadership role. Only 25% strongly agree young women are supported to develop management or leadership skills, against Girlguiding's 71% of women aged 17–21 who want to lead. And 44% of managers believe mentoring or sponsorship would help identify emerging female talent — the room knows the fix and still doesn't have it. → [[2026-11-19|Radar]] → [[sponsorship-gap]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]] → [[pipeline-and-education]]
- AI expands her agency, not her visibility. BairesDev's Dev Barometer Q1 2026 (1,329 developers, 61 countries, 25% women): 64% of women developers say AI improved their ability to shape their career direction and 84% report stronger career momentum linked to AI skills — but only 33% say AI adoption increased their visibility or access to opportunities. Confidence in the gender fairness of AI-supported tools runs 56% vs 64% for men, and women name structural levers for advancement: flexible work (36%), transparent promotion criteria (27%), active sponsorship (16%). → [[2026-11-19|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] → [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]
- The AI-leadership seat wave reaches state government — and the seat's first holder lasted about a month. Illinois rehired former CDO Tammy Roust as its second-ever chief AI officer; predecessor Kader Sakkaria, named the inaugural CAIO in June, departed after about a month in post. Roust brings 25+ years of AI, data and cybersecurity leadership and most recently founded FAIT Consulting. The fourth woman into an AI-leadership seat this autumn, and the first in government. Not seated on an appointment alone. → [[2026-11-19|Radar]] → [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]]
