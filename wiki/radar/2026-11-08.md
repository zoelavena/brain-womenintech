## Decisions for the curator

1. **The AI catch-up grows again — Fortune's "climbing wall" (Nov 2, missed by the Nov 7 run).** Microsoft's Aneesh Raman says AI is killing the career ladder and replacing it with a "climbing wall" — progress not always upward, paths charted by the worker. The catch, named in the piece itself: less predictable advancement could disadvantage women and other historically marginalised groups, who research says benefit from clear criteria for raises and promotions. Raman's answer is "radical transparency." The piece also carries a new Pew receipt from researcher Nicole Smith: in more AI-exposed occupations, men's employment grew 6.3% for each one-point increase in an occupation's AI-exposure score, while women's barely changed; among workers with a high school diploma or less, women's employment fell (no percentage given). Raman, by contrast, predicts more women CEOs and board representation rising from roughly a third to half or more, on women's edge in empathy and judgement. Read in full via the live browser (fortune.com is blocked to the text fetcher). This speaks directly to the brain's open questions — what the ladder becomes, and whether the door narrows more for women — and the 6.3% figure would be the brain's first *observed* AI-exposure employment effect, next to the Philadelphia Fed's scenario analysis. File it with the pending Pew/Shorenstein/Danish AI catch-up, or leave the recency window standing?
2. **LinkedIn's *The Triple Penalty* (July/August 2026) — catch-up?** The brain filed the headline Nov 2 (13% of C-suite AI roles globally, 26% of new US AI hires), but the report's decomposition is new to the brain: women are ~15 percentage points less represented in C-suite roles than in non-executive roles, ~10 points less represented in AI jobs than in non-AI jobs, and ~5 points less represented at AI companies than at non-AI firms. Role-level hire shares: Head of AI 20% women, Director of AI 26%, Member of Technical Staff 18% — while data annotator, the lowest-paid AI role, is the most gender-balanced (over half women). Australia: 15% of C-suite AI roles at AI companies. The pay split behind it: AI workers average $177,000 a year vs $80,000 for non-AI roles (ACS/Information Age, Aug 2026; the ACS page itself carries no visible date, dated here from Aug 19–25 secondary coverage). File the decomposition as an enrichment of the Nov 2 filing, or leave it?
3. **I Wish 2026 survey (Ireland, Oct 22–23) — catch-up?** The 11th annual survey of female students' attitudes to STEM: 60% of girls cite a lack of confidence in their STEM subject ability as a barrier, up from 55% in 2025 and 50% in 2024 — a three-year climb. Only 27% of girls in single-sex schools have access to Leaving Cert computer science, vs 60% in mixed-sex schools; 66% report a lack of information about STEM careers, 61% a lack of school visits by female STEM role models; two-thirds identify poor gender equality in STEM careers as a barrier. Taoiseach Micheál Martin at the launch: women are one fifth of Ireland's digital-specialist workforce, against demand projected to rise 27% in four years. The confidence-gap trend and the access gap speak to node 3.2 and the pipeline theme, and the framing is explicitly about AI careers ("Girls at risk of being left behind in AI careers"). Figures as reported in secondary coverage (Bytes EU, Apex News); the I Wish report itself was not opened. File it in a catch-up, or leave it?
4. **Still open from Nov 7, unanswered:** the ILO *Women in Business and Management: Scaling Success* catch-up, the Pew/Shorenstein/Danish AI catch-up (to which decision 1 above now attaches), and the *Investing in Women Code Annual Report 2026* catch-up.
