## 1. The harms aren't a list. They're a cascade.

The Victorian Women's Trust's *Gender and AI: Where's the Harm?* (October 2026) is the first known attempt in Australia to map gendered harms of AI at a systems level. Led by Dr Elise Stephenson, the Trust's inaugural Feminist Researcher in Residence, it identifies 20 fundamental harms that disproportionately impact women, girls and gender diverse people — and its framing is the point: the harms are not isolated incidents but a cascading system, where exclusion from AI design, ownership and governance amplifies multiple downstream harms simultaneously.

- **The bias is measured across systems, not one model.** A study of 133 AI systems found 44% demonstrated gender bias, and more than a quarter showed both gender and racial bias. *(VWT, citing UN News, June 22, 2026 — verified at UN News: "44 per cent demonstrated gender bias, while more than a quarter showed both gender and racial bias.")* The same UN piece adds that about one in five LLM sentence completions came back sexist or misogynistic, and that only 24 of 138 countries refer to gender in their national AI strategies.
- **The labour cluster lands on 3.5.** 15 of the 20 occupations most exposed to automation are female-dominated (Jobs and Skills Australia, via the VWT media release), and the snapshot names biased recruiting systems that suppress wages and amplify pay gaps, alongside female-dominated occupations disproportionately automated and reinforced occupational segregation. That is a mechanism claim about how AI touches hiring and pay — filed as such, not as a payroll figure. New from the snapshot PDF: a 2025 Australian study testing OpenAI and Microsoft models as preferred job candidates in software engineering found the models favoured male and Caucasian profiles, especially in senior roles; the Australian tech sector's total remuneration gender pay gap runs significantly higher than the economy-wide average, and nearly half of women in STEM report harassment at five times the rate reported by men; and the "ghost work" behind AI — violent-content data-labelling workers, often outsourced to the Global South — is traumatising and low-paid. *(VWT Research Snapshot, Oct 2026, "Unpacking the gendered AI harms")*
- **Who builds it is the taproot.** 88% of leading AI researchers globally are male *(UN, Preliminary Report of the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, 2026)*; women are 30% of Australia's AI industry *(Deloitte Access Economics and Australian Computer Society, Digital Pulse 2026)* and 30% of the global AI workforce *(ILO, via UN News, June 2026)*. Only 2% of all venture capital for startups went to female-only teams in 2025 *(Cut Through Venture and Folklore Ventures, State of Australian Startup Funding 2025)*.
- **Gendered digital exclusion is the feedback loop.** Women show lower uptake of AI tools than men, which the snapshot ties to a skills and AI-literacy gap, reduced participation in AI careers, and economic loss where AI skills are valued in jobs — and women turning away from tech (distrust, ethical concerns, lack of interest) means women's inputs don't train the next models. *(VWT Research Snapshot, Oct 2026, "Unpacking the gendered AI harms")*
- **What comes next.** A full White Paper lands in early 2027, examining five priority harm areas in greater detail: agency and autonomy, social and relational harms, political and democratic exclusion, accountability and governance gaps, and environmental and intergenerational impacts.

Five of the 20 harms are named as example clusters on the VWT page: labour market inequality, disruption and job losses; civic and political exclusion; unchecked AI action; technology-facilitated gender-based violence; and under-representation and exclusion from AI.

## 🧠 The brain's read

This is the first source in the brain that treats AI harms as a system with a taproot rather than a list of incidents — and the taproot is the one the tree already knows: exclusion from the rooms where the thing gets built. The JHU prompt study showed bias baked into the drafting tool; the LinkedIn hiring flow showed it baked into who gets the AI jobs; this snapshot says the same exclusion multiplies across work and voice at once. The labour numbers are the ones that land hardest for this brain: 15 of the 20 occupations most exposed to automation are female-dominated, the recruiting models themselves favoured male profiles for senior software roles, and the tech pay gap runs higher than the economy-wide average. And the digital-exclusion loop is new here: women using AI tools less, turning away from tech, and then their inputs missing from the models that decide the next round. The other thing this filing does is put a date on the next chapter — the White Paper in early 2027 — so the cascade frame gets tested against deeper evidence soon.
