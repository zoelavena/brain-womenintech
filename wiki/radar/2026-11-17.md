| 2 | [It's the choice, not the preparation: the STEM gap, decomposed across ten countries](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cep:cepdps:dp2120): women are 35% of global STEM graduates, a share unchanged for a decade. Using administrative microdata from centralised university admissions in ten systems (Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Finland, Greece, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan, Uganda), the paper splits the gap into a pipeline gap (academic preparedness) and a choice gap (first-choice field conditional on eligibility). The pipeline gap varies widely, from −19 to +31 percentage points across systems. The choice gap is remarkably stable: even among top scorers, women are about 25 percentage points less likely than men to rank STEM first — women were 51.2% of top-achieving applicants but only 34% of the high-achieving applicants who prioritised a STEM programme, and the gap exceeds 20 points in eight of the ten settings. Closing the pipeline gap entirely would still leave high-achieving STEM applicants majority male (57–75%) in every setting. The authors do not identify mechanisms directly — stereotypes, role models and perceptions of STEM careers are their named candidates. Catch-up: CEP Discussion Paper 2120, Aug 27, 2025; updated version Oct 28, 2026 (SOFI Working Papers 12/2026), conditionally accepted by Economics of Education Review. ## Curator decisions
1. **The choice gap is now the pipeline theme's mechanism.** The LSE paper answers the theme's Still brewing question ("We need a source on *why* fewer women choose STEM") in part: it's choice, not preparation, and closing preparation alone wouldn't fix it. Does the question move from open to answered-in-part, and does the choice-vs-pipeline split deserve its own concept page?
2. **Track employer inaction as a series?** The Omni survey's "30% found a gap and did nothing" is the sharpest measurement yet of the incentives question — sharper than Ravio's "44% took no action" because it starts from companies that *found* a gap. Worth a recurring receipt (next Omni survey, or a pay-equity-review practice metric)?
3. **Seat Tanna?** Three women now hold brand-new CAIO seats (Larkin, Shaver, Tanna), and Tanna is the most substantive of the three — first CAIO at a major bank, with a public statement of her brief. But it is still an appointment alone, the same call as Shaver/Larkin. And Dani Matthews (Abundium CEO, advocate) and Jane Huxley (former Are Media/Spotify MD) spoke on sponsorship with substance tonight — one summit quote each, not a profile. Seat any of them, or hold the line?
4. Remaining open catch-ups: Fortune climbing wall, B.C. pay transparency, Eurostat S&E series, Senate TechWomen letter, NSF DOJ response, ILO board-chair discrepancy.
