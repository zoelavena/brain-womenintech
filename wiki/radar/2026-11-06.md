## 1. Representation is falling, not flat

Ravio's companion page to the pay-gap analysis filed Nov 4 turns the same dataset (Compensation Trends 2027: live data from 1,800+ companies and 618,000+ employee datapoints, plus a survey of 150 People and Reward Leaders) onto representation — and the headline is a three-year trend the brain didn't have: women were 41% of the European tech workforce in 2024, 38% in 2025, and 36% in 2026.

- **The ladder, by level:** women hold 40% of professional (individual contributor) roles, 36% of management roles, and 22% of executive roles. The share falls at every step up.
- **By function:** Engineering is lowest at 19% (20% in both 2024 and 2025 — flat, not falling). People (69%) and Marketing (55%) have the most women. Sales saw the sharpest drop this year, from 42% to 33%.
- **By country:** the UK is highest at 41% and held steady; Germany and the Netherlands are lowest at 34%. Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden saw the sharpest dips this year (4–6 percentage points).
- **By pay quartile:** women are 48% of the lowest-paid quarter and 25% of the highest-paid quarter — the same split filed Nov 4 from the pay page, now with the full quartile table (Q2 40%, Q3 30%).
- **The mechanism, in Ravio's own framing:** underrepresentation in senior roles (36% of People and Reward leaders) and in higher-paying functions like Engineering (26%) are the top named drivers of the pay gap — the same numbers filed Nov 4, now attached to the representation trend that explains them. Ravio's own caveat, kept here: "Year-to-year changes like this are within the range of normal fluctuation, so they don't necessarily signal a downward trend — but representation certainly isn't improving."

**Voice heard, not seated:** Matt McFarlane (Director at FNDN) and Liis Reitz (Compensation Specialist, SMG Swiss Marketplace Group) are quoted on transparency and promotion patterns, and Andreea Lupu (Compensation & Benefits Architect at Meaningful) on job postings written toward a male profile. Single quotes each, on their employer's pay practices — heard, not seated.

## 2. One year on from the Lovelace Report: the promotion clock, and who's getting the AI work

The Lovelace Report — WeAreTechWomen and Oliver Wyman, launched July 15, 2025 at the House of Commons, from 500+ mid-to-senior women surveyed, 20 senior technologists interviewed, and Mercer Comptryx data benchmarked across 600+ UK tech-focused firms — was the brain's missing UK retention receipt. Prolific North's one-year-on piece (Nov 4, 2026) with co-author Vanessa Vallely OBE supplies the numbers and the update:

- **The leak has a price:** an estimated 40,000–60,000 women decide to leave their tech and digital jobs every year — some leaving the industry, some moving between jobs — costing the UK tech sector between £2bn and £3.5bn a year in replacement costs and lost productivity.
- **The promotion clock:** "We know from the Lovelace report that men get promoted in two years. For women, it's four." Pay disparities reach up to £50,000. Promotion delays sit "at every single juncture of a woman's career."
- **She pays for her own development:** 70% of the women surveyed were paying out of their own pockets to develop their own careers, rather than receiving the support they needed from employers.
- **The trope that didn't hold:** only 3% of respondents cited childcare as an issue holding them back. Vallely's read: the cost of childcare is the problem, not childcare as a reason to stall a career — "That shouldn't be a reason for us to not progress in our careers because we choose to have children." Filed as a ⬇️ wrinkle in [[caregiving-and-social-norms]]' Still brewing, not as a receipt: it complicates the mechanism, it doesn't settle it.
- **What's moved since the report:** around 5,000 employers have received it; the UK government launched the national Women in Tech Taskforce at the end of 2025, citing the report; the government-funded TechFirst Women's Programme launches in Manchester on Nov 10, 2026, aiming to train and support at least 300 women into paid tech roles by March 2027; and Vallely's new book, *404 Women Not Found*, follows the report's findings.
- **The AI reframing:** "AI has totally changed the landscape. When we did the report, we weren't in the AI fire that we are now." Her co-author Deborah O'Neill's line from the House of Commons: "It isn't so much on who's getting promoted anymore. It's who's getting the AI work." Vallely's warning: "If we don't make sure that women are given the opportunities to be part of those big AI projects, if our voices are not in the room, we will just perpetuate the bias in the systems, the processes and the things that we build in the future." And: "If anything, it could be an additional barrier as we push towards this AI landscape. If the women are not there, it's going to push us further down the stack."
- **The thesis, in her words:** "It is not about fixing women. We don't need fixing. It's the systems which we operate in that do." And the long-game framing: "There are not enough women at senior or c-suite level. It's a long game because you have to build."

**Voice seated:** [[vanessa-vallely|Vanessa Vallely]] OBE — co-founder of WeAreTechWomen, co-author of the Lovelace Report, author of *404 Women Not Found* — seated at the Tea Party as brewing, advocate. **Voice heard, not seated:** Liz Rhodes, CEO of Leeds-based tech firm Art of Cloud, quoted on pulling down barriers in her own business — a single passing mention, no profile.

## 🧠 The brain's read

Ravio gives the brain its first multi-year representation decline: 41 → 38 → 36. Every other representation receipt in the brain was a point in time or a single drop; this is three points in the same dataset, all pointing down, in the same week the pay page said equal work is nearly equally paid. Put together, the story sharpens into something the diary has been circling: the pay gap is now a representation gap, and the representation gap is widening. Ravio itself hedges — year-to-year moves may be fluctuation — but "representation certainly isn't improving" is the publisher's own sentence, and the country splits (Germany 45 → 34 in two years) make the flat-reading hard to hold.

The Lovelace numbers land on the same week as Ensono's and Ravio's, and they rhyme: promotion takes twice as long, women pay for their own development, and the sector pays £2bn–£3.5bn a year for the exits. But the piece's sharpest contribution isn't a number — it's Vallely moving the question. The brain has been asking who gets promoted; she says the question now is who gets the AI work. That's the exposure gap with a new name, and it connects 3.4 to 3.1 in a way the tree didn't have: the AI programmes are the resource now, and being left off them is how you get pushed "further down the stack." The 3% childcare figure is the honest complication: the brain carries caregiving as a central mechanism (Ensono: 49% call it the leading in-office challenge), and a 500-woman survey says almost nobody named childcare as the blocker. Both can be true if the cost is the mechanism and the citation is the symptom — but that's a guess, and it's recorded as one in Still brewing, not as a conclusion.
