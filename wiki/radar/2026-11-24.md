| 1 | [The average says no bias. The occupations say otherwise](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.2534118123): Elass, Gauthier, Profeta and Nozza (PNAS, Nov 16, 2026) ran a preregistered audit of six language models — GPT 5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, DeepSeek 3.2, Mistral Large 3, Grok 4.1, Llama 3.3 70B — across 98 US occupations, generating 35,280 CVs with gender randomly assigned, then asking the models to score candidates and recommend salaries. The average screening gap is close to zero and not statistically significant — but split by occupation it reverses: in occupations where women are under 10% of the workforce, women score about 2.7% lower (1.6 points evaluation bias, 1.1 generation bias) and are 4.8 percentage points less likely to pass the initial screen; where women are over 80%, they gain a similar advantage. The CVs themselves carry the stereotype: written for women, they lean on helping others and non-promotable tasks, and a word-count classifier still recovers the intended gender 81% of the time with names and pronouns stripped. Salary advice for explicitly identified women runs about 1.6% lower, with bigger penalties where the observed pay gap is wider. Telling the model to be gender-neutral leaves the bias largely intact. This is Fawcett's Nov 20 warning — AI pay-setting systems reproducing historical bias — with an experimental receipt behind it, and it answers the AI theme's open question (does AI in hiring reduce the bias at 3.5, or bake it in?) with: it bakes it in, twice over — once in the drafting, once in the screening. | 2 | [Who decides what AI is built to do: 19% of the room, none of the CTOs](https://www.winstontaylor.com/insights/women-in-ai-from-representation-to-influence): Winston Taylor's *Women in AI: From Representation to Influence* (Nov 19, 2026) counts leadership across the 2026 Forbes AI 50, the FTSE 100 and the S&P 100. At the AI 50, women hold 19% of recorded senior leadership roles, 6% of founder-titled roles and 8% of CEO roles (four of the 50 CEOs); 40% of the companies list no women on their leadership teams at all; and among 34 identified CTO positions, none is held by a woman. The one place women are near parity is legal and counsel roles, at 46.2%. The public-company split is wide: women are 29.6% of identified FTSE 100 AI leaders vs 16.9% at the S&P 100. The report's own prescription names the gap the brain keeps measuring: a seat isn't enough — women need control over budgets, hiring, and product and risk decisions, "to decide which problems an AI system tackles, how it is tested, and whether it is ready to be used." That is the ISACA authority gap (decision rights lagging 13–20 points behind progression) wearing the AI industry's clothes — and it sharpens the CAIO test: the 13% of C-suite AI roles receipt now sits beside a 0-of-34 CTO count.
