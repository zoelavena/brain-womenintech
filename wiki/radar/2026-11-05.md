## 1. The skills are up. So is the workload. So is the burnout.

Ensono's sixth-annual Speak Up survey (1,500 female-identifying technology professionals across the US, UK and India, released Nov 5, 2026) is the first survey in this brain to catch women in tech *after* the AI skill-up — and the picture is two-sided.

- **The skill-up is real:** 70% rate their generative AI knowledge and skills as "advanced" or "expert," with those identifying as experts doubling since the 2024 survey.
- **The workload went up with it:** two-thirds say AI has made their workload more manageable, but 42% say they are expected to produce more work in less time because of AI. Add pressure to always be available (34%) and to constantly develop new skills to remain competitive (43%), and the efficiency gain is being banked by the employer, not the employee.
- **Burnout is the baseline:** 87% report at least some level of burnout; 60% describe themselves as moderately, very, or extremely burned out.
- **The office is back, and it costs:** 53% say their organizations require more in-office days than a year ago, and 92% agree being visible in person is important for career advancement. Balancing caregiving responsibilities with work is the leading challenge associated with in-person work (49%) — and women in India are far more likely than US and UK peers to experience both the in-person visibility and caregiving challenges.
- **Ambition is being redefined, not abandoned:** 83% say they aspire to advance into an executive leadership position — but 43% are increasingly content in their current positions, and among those not pursuing executive leadership, preference for hands-on technical work rose to 41% (from 35% last year). Nearly half say building skills and expertise (49%) and maintaining work-life balance (48%) have become bigger career priorities over the past year. Work-life balance is the most important workplace factor (40%) and a top reason for not pursuing leadership roles (33%).

**Voice heard, not seated:** Meredith Graham, Chief People Officer at Ensono, quoted in the release on clarity around how AI changes work expectations and on supporting flexibility and different paths to advancement. A company spokesperson on her own company's survey, not a woman-in-tech voice with public work on a theme. No profile created.

## 2. Three women, one mechanism: somebody has to say her name

FinTech Futures interviewed the three nominees for the Woman in Technology award at the Banking Tech Awards 2026 (ceremony Dec 3, 2026), and all three landed on the same mechanism from different angles.

- **Julie Chatterjee**, group CEO of Sweden's Northmill Bank since August 2024, has driven AI across software development, internal operations and customer services — including 2,000+ tailored welcome journeys — and an AI-literacy push for employees. Her thesis: "When organisations break down technical gatekeeping and give women across all functions the confidence to build with new technology, we naturally create a stronger pipeline into executive roles." On advancement: banks must "promote people based on real, measurable impact rather than familiarity."
- **Ammara Masood**, who leads the global banking and financial services business at Systems Limited (Pakistan) after nearly 15 years as CEO of NdcTech, names the retention cliff in emerging markets: "The problem in developing countries like ours is that women enter the industry in the early part of their career, but rarely sustain their career in terms of progressing and going into leadership roles" — with frequent international travel in consulting work making it harder for women with family responsibilities to continue. She says she personally works to make sure women "come up the ladder at different stages and do not get sidelined."
- **Yuan Gao**, founder and CEO of London fintech Pangura (founded April 2025, after Swiss Re and CICC), holds a PhD in engineering and personally coded the platform's initial version. Her ask is to "widen the door" for women from technical disciplines into fintech — and her sponsorship line is the cleanest in the piece: "If someone senior puts a woman's name forward for such a role, that is the opportunity."

**Voices heard, not seated:** all three have real public work and substantive views, but each is known to the brain from a single awards-preview interview. No profiles created; see the curator decision below on whether Chatterjee — group CEO, AI adoption across software development, an explicit gatekeeping-to-pipeline thesis — merits a Tea Party seat.

## 3. The pushback has a price tag (filed as 🌱 Didn't fit)

The Commonwealth Secretariat's *The Pushback against Gender Equality and Women's Empowerment in the Commonwealth*, launched at the Commonwealth Women's Forum in Antigua and Barbuda on Nov 4, 2026, estimates cross-border transnational spending on efforts to challenge and oppose women's rights, gender equality and inclusion at **US$1.18 billion between 2019 and 2023**. Nearly a quarter of governments worldwide reported a rise in coordinated opposition to gender equality (per a 2025 UNRISD/UN Women report cited in it). The report names rising sexism, harassment and violence against women in public life (including online abuse), cuts to institutions supporting gender equality, rigid expectations around unpaid care, and resistance to recognising climate impacts on women and girls.

This is a general gender-equality report, not a women-in-tech source, so it fits no theme and no receipt was filed. It is recorded here because it rhymes with a pattern already in the brain — DEI pullback tracking with women feeling less safe to raise gender issues (Web Summit 2026: 59% vs 29%) — and because it prices the opposition: $1.18 billion of organized, funded pushback against the very progress the brain tracks. Whether the brain follows the pushback as context, or keeps it out of scope as non-tech, is a curator decision.

## 🧠 The brain's read

Ensono is the receipts version of the thesis the Nov 4 run wrote in words: AI is adding workload instead of relief. Two-thirds say AI made the workload more manageable — and 42% are still expected to produce more in less time. Both can be true when the saved time is immediately re-spent. The burnout number (87% at least some) next to the skill number (70% advanced/expert) is the sharpest pairing tonight: women are doing the learning the AI wave demands, and the wave is not giving the time back.

The in-office numbers extend the flexibility-tax pattern a third dataset already named: 92% say in-person visibility matters for advancement, and 53% face more in-office days than a year ago, with caregiving the leading in-office challenge (49%). The tax is no longer just about remote work being quietly penalised — it's about presence being explicitly rewarded while the care load stays put.

And the ambition redefinition is worth watching rather than worrying about: 83% still aspire to executive leadership, but 43% are content where they are and hands-on technical work is gaining as a chosen path (41%, up from 35%). Against the tree, that reads less like women opting out and more like the ladder being measured against a different ruler — skills and balance — when the ladder's own rewards (visibility, sponsorship, the exec seat) are still gated by presence and familiarity. Gao's line — someone senior putting a woman's name forward — is the same mechanism the sponsorship theme has carried since 2010, now spoken by a founder who coded her own platform.
