# 🛰️ Radar: 2026-11-22

Scheduled nightly run. Window: everything published since the Nov 21 run. A quiet Sunday on the news front, so tonight is mostly catch-up — and the catch-up is the story: the double bind from last night now has a name, a mechanism, and a real workplace behind it. Harvard Business Review's hidden-penalty experiment (a year old, missed until now) shows women engineers who use AI are rated 13% less competent by peers for identical code, against 6% for men — and the harshest judges are the men who don't use AI themselves. LeanIn's new synthesis adds the praise gap on top: men are 27% more likely to be praised for using AI, women 32% more likely to worry they'll be seen as cheating. And a new voice arrives at the Tea Party: Tara Chklovski, who has spent 20 years teaching girls to build technology, says women are "the single largest group that would be left behind" in AI — because norms move slower than the tools.

| 1 | [The hidden penalty of using AI at work](https://hbr.org/2025/08/research-the-hidden-penalty-of-using-ai-at-work): a pre-registered experiment with 1,026 engineers at one large tech company (28,698 engineers studied overall) finds reviewers rated identical Python code 9% lower on competence when they believed AI had assisted — and the penalty was more than twice as severe for women engineers, 13% vs 6% for men. The harshest judges were the non-adopters: male engineers who don't use AI themselves penalised female colleagues who used it 26% more harshly than they penalised men for identical AI use. A follow-up survey of 919 engineers found women and older engineers anticipated the penalty and avoided AI to protect their reputations — and adoption at the company was just 41% overall, 31% among women. The cost: a net loss of at least 2.5% of annual profit, potentially as high as 14%. The authors' prescription is not more training but redesigning evaluation: blind reviews, outcome metrics instead of method tags, and visible senior women using AI openly. | Harvard Business Review · Aug 1, 2025 | [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] · 3.1, [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]] · 3.5 | ⬆️ | — |
| 2 | [The praise gap, the cheating worry, and the encouragement gap](https://leanin.org/articles/findings/why-women-use-ai-less-than-men/): LeanIn.Org's synthesis of its March 2026 survey (1,000+ Americans) and other research finds women are 32% more likely than men to worry they'll be perceived as cheating when they use AI — and the fear isn't baseless: men who used AI at work were 27% more likely to have been praised for doing so than women. Deloitte's numbers run the same way: 93% of men vs 84% of women say their company actively encourages AI use, and 91% of men vs 72% of women have received formal AI training. Women are 22% less likely than men to use AI regularly at work, and only 21% of entry-level women are encouraged by their manager to use AI vs 33% of men (Women in the Workplace 2025). The synthesis also notes women are almost twice as likely as men to predict more women than men will be laid off due to AI, and that people who use AI are twice as likely to be promoted. | LeanIn.Org · Nov 2026 | [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] · 3.1, [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]] · 3.5 | ⬆️ | — |
| 3 | [Women are 86% of the workers most exposed to AI job loss](https://leanin.org/articles/findings/why-women-use-ai-less-than-men/): a 2026 Washington Post analysis of jobs most threatened by AI found women make up 86% of workers who are highly exposed to AI job loss — and the least able to adapt to it. The exposure story now has both ends: the jobs most exposed are women's, and the growth in AI-exposed occupations went almost entirely to men (Pew, Oct 22). | Washington Post, via LeanIn.Org · March 2026 | [[exposure-gap]] · 3.4 | ⬆️ | — |
| 4 | [Tara Chklovski: women are "the single largest group that would be left behind" in AI](https://www.einpresswire.com/article-print/958054852/after-20-years-technovation-ceo-tara-chklovski-tells-tomorrow-today-why-women-risk-falling-behind-in-ai): in a long-form interview on Tomorrow, Today (released Nov 22, 2026), the founder and CEO of Technovation — the global nonprofit teaching girls technology and entrepreneurship, started in 2006 as Iridescent — says social norms are moving more slowly than the technology, and that is what leaves women behind. Her numbers: a community of 300,000 women, about 33,000 girls in the programme this season, roughly 70% of participants going on to technology or other STEM careers, and a goal of reskilling 25 million young people — the modelled critical mass for the experience to spread on its own in each country. Technovation has taught AI since 2016. Her mechanism is courage as a trainable skill: teamwork, mentors, and building something for someone else — and her prescription is that women help build better AI rather than only critique it. | EIN Presswire / Tomorrow, Today · Nov 22, 2026 | [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] · 3.1, [[pipeline-and-education]] · 2.1 | ⬆️ | [[tara-chklovski\|Tara Chklovski]] |
| 5 | [Belonging is separate from interest: half of women engineering students felt the field was "not for someone like me"](https://www.ukesf.org/wp-content/uploads/UKESF-Pathways-for-Women-into-EEE-Report-2026.pdf): the UK Electronics Skills Foundation's *Pathways into Electronics: Women EEE Students* (Oct 2026; survey of 149 women students across 28 UK universities, summer 2026) finds women are just 14% of Electronic and Electrical Engineering students, even as women have broken the 20% barrier across BEng degrees overall. Nearly half of respondents said EEE had, at some point, felt "not for someone like me" — and these were students who had entered the field, not rejected it. Interest is not the problem: 81% cited enjoyment of physics, maths, electronics or design & technology as a motivation. The decision comes late — 76.5% decided during or after A-Levels/Highers — but the confidence behind it is built earlier: 81% had taken part in at least one exposure activity (taster days, work experience, coding courses, girls-in-STEM events, clubs, personal projects). Small, UK-only, self-selected sample — a belonging signal, not a census. | UKESF · Oct 2026 | [[pipeline-and-education]] · 2.1 | ⬆️ | — |

The Nov 21 double bind was a vignette; this is a working engineering org. Is the penalty a fourth leg of the adoption-gap story, or the same visibility tax in an evaluation outfit?
