# Beatrice Magistro

> 🪑 **Seat:** researcher · **Known for:** Assistant professor of AI governance; the peer-reviewed test of why women are more skeptical of AI · **Shows up at:** [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] (3.1) · [[evidence-gap]]

## ☕ Her tea

Girl, she ran the experiment the adoption-gap story was missing: it's not that women can't use the tools, it's that they weigh what the tools do to the world — and when the employment outlook turns uncertain, their support falls faster than men's. One honest flag: this profile rests on one peer-reviewed paper and one interview — that's the record so far, and it's why she's brewing, not steeped.

## 👩🏽‍💻 Who she is
- Assistant professor of AI governance at Northeastern University. *(Northeastern Global News, Jan 22, 2026)*
- Co-author, with Sophie Borwein, R. Michael Alvarez, Bart Bonikowski and Peter J. Loewen, of "Explaining women's skepticism toward artificial intelligence: The role of risk orientation and risk exposure," published in *PNAS Nexus* on January 20, 2026. *(PNAS Nexus 5(1), pgaf399)*
- The study surveyed ~3,049 respondents across Canada and the United States (YouGov opt-in panel, November 2023), measuring risk orientation with lottery questions and risk exposure through education and occupation. *(PNAS Nexus, Jan 20, 2026)*

## 🍵 Her stories
- **The two drivers:** women consistently perceive AI as riskier than men — 11% more likely to say its risks outweigh its benefits (4.87 vs 4.38 on a 0–10 scale) — and the paper traces that to two drivers: women's higher general risk aversion and their greater exposure to AI-related employment risks. *(PNAS Nexus, Jan 20, 2026)*
- **The causal test:** the survey experiment varied the probability that a company's AI adoption would create more jobs than it destroys. As that probability fell, women's support for adoption fell more sharply than men's — but when net benefits were guaranteed, women supported adoption nearly as strongly as men. *(PNAS Nexus, Jan 20, 2026)*
- **The uncertainty:** in open-ended answers, women were 6–7 points more likely to say they "don't know" what AI's benefits are, and 2–3 points more likely to say they are "not sure" or that there are no benefits. *(PNAS Nexus, Jan 20, 2026)*
