# Kenza Elass

> 🪑 **Seat:** researcher · **Known for:** Lead author of the PNAS audit showing AI resume tools penalise women in male-dominated jobs — on average, not at all · **Shows up at:** [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]] (3.1) · [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]

## ☕ Her tea

Girl, she ran the audit that catches the average lying: six AI models, 35,280 CVs, and the screening gap is zero on average — until you split by occupation, where women lose 2.7% of the score and 4.8 points of pass chance in the jobs with the fewest women. Her line is the one to carry into every salary conversation: the number the assistant suggests becomes the number you negotiate from. One honest flag: this profile rests on one peer-reviewed paper and one university write-up — that's the record so far, and it's why she's brewing, not steeped.

## 👩🏽‍💻 Who she is
- Fellow at Bocconi University's Research Lab on Gender Equality, and researcher at ENS de Lyon. *(Bocconi University, Nov 17, 2026)*
- Lead author, with Germain Gauthier, Paola Profeta and Debora Nozza, of "Automating inequality? Gender bias in AI-mediated labor markets," published in *PNAS* on November 16, 2026. *(PNAS, Nov 16, 2026)*
- The study is a preregistered audit experiment (American Economic Association registry, AEARCTR-0013538) across six language models and 98 US occupations. *(PNAS, Nov 16, 2026)*

## 🍵 Her stories
- **The double filter:** the models write gender-stereotypical CVs — more references to helping others and to tasks unlikely to lead to promotion for women — and then a second system scores them. Even with names and pronouns stripped, a word-count classifier recovers the intended gender 81% of the time. *(PNAS, Nov 16, 2026; Bocconi University, Nov 17, 2026)*
- **The average that hides the edges:** pooled across all 98 occupations, the screening gap is close to zero and not statistically significant. In occupations where women are under 10% of the workforce, women score about 2.7% lower and are 4.8 percentage points less likely to pass the initial screen; where women are over 80%, they gain a similar advantage. *(PNAS, Nov 16, 2026)*
- **The salary advice:** when gender is explicitly stated, the models' salary recommendations for women run about 1.6% lower on average, with bigger penalties in professions where the observed pay gap is wider. *(PNAS, Nov 16, 2026; Bocconi University, Nov 17, 2026)*
- **The instruction that doesn't fix it:** asking the model to be strictly gender-neutral leaves the biases largely intact; asking it to favour female candidates produces a strong, generalised correction rather than one targeted at the imbalances. *(Bocconi University, Nov 17, 2026)*

- **She believes** automation doesn't automatically make hiring fairer — a system can accurately describe the labour market and, precisely for that reason, become problematic when used to guide decisions. 🟡 *(my read from the paper's argument — the "description to recommendation" point her co-author Germain Gauthier states in the Bocconi write-up, Nov 17, 2026)*

- **Her instinct when something's unfair** is to audit it: randomise the gender, hold everything else constant, and let the split — not the average — tell the story. 🟡 *(my read from the paper's design — a preregistered audit across 98 occupations, built to separate generation bias from evaluation bias)*
