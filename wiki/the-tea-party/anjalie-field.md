# Anjalie Field

> 🪑 **Seat:** 🔬 researcher · **Known for:** Studying how AI performs for different people — bias in language models, from the user's side · **Shows up at:** [[ai-and-the-future-of-women-in-tech]], [[hiring-and-promotion-bias]]

## ☕ Her tea

Most bias research asks what AI models say about people. Anjalie Field asks how the models treat the people using them — and found the answer depends on how you talk, not who you are. She's the researcher who moved the bias question from the output to the user.

## 👩🏽‍💻 Who she is

- Assistant Professor of Computer Science at Johns Hopkins University (since August 2023), affiliated with the Center for Language and Speech Processing (CLSP) and the Data Science and AI Institute. *(JHU Engineering faculty page; AI2050 profile)*
- PhD, Language Technologies Institute, Carnegie Mellon University (2022), advised by Yulia Tsvetkov; postdoctoral researcher in the Stanford NLP Group and Stanford Data Science Institute with Dan Jurafsky and Jennifer Eberhardt; visiting student at the University of Washington, 2021–2022; BA in Computer Science, Princeton University. *(JHU Engineering; Stanford Data Science)*
- Before graduate school, she worked for a few years as a software developer; she went back to school to work on projects with a positive impact on society. *(JHU faculty Q&A)*
- Research: the ethics and social science aspects of natural language processing — developing models to address discrimination and propaganda, and critically assessing privacy, transparency, and fairness in AI pipelines. Published in ACL and PNAS; named an AI2050 Early Career Fellow by Schmidt Sciences in 2024; Wikimedia Foundation Research Award of the Year 2023 (per her CV). *(AI2050; JHU Engineering)*
- Teaches "AI Ethics and Social Impact" (graduate seminar) and "NLP for Computational Social Science." *(JHU faculty Q&A)*

## 🍵 Her stories

- **The user's side of bias.** Earlier bias research mostly examined what models produce — stereotypes in generated text, biased depictions. Field's prompt-register study with Katherine Van Koevering flipped the question: how do models perform *for different users*? Rewriting real workplace prompts with women- vs men-associated language, they found women-associated prompts drew shorter, less sophisticated, less formal drafts from GPT-4, Llama, Gemma, and Mistral alike — and a male sign-off name changed virtually nothing. *(JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026; COLM 2026 paper)*
- **High-stakes domains.** A long-running project with a child protective services agency investigates where NLP can increase harm: what existing data biases models amplify, where the technology deepens power imbalances, and whether privacy can be preserved in research and development. Earlier work examined risks of racial bias in NLP tools for child protective services (FAccT 2023) and surveyed race, racism, and anti-racism in NLP (ACL 2021). *(JHU faculty Q&A; publication record)*
- **The three-direction agenda.** She describes her research as three intersecting directions: building NLP methods to identify social issues like stereotyping and propaganda; building NLP for social good in public service domains; and turning the lens back on her own field to examine the harms of NLP and AI. The directions overlap on purpose: social-good NLP has to be ethical, and identifying bias needs social science perspectives on what bias is. *(JHU faculty Q&A)*

## 🔮 How I think she thinks

> The brain's perceived profile, read from her public work. 🟢 = she said it (cited). 🟡 = my read, inferred from the cited work. It's not her words.

- **She believes the user's side is the understudied side.** Most bias work looks at what models depict; far less looks at how models perform for different groups of people, or even different individuals. 🟢 *(JHU Hub coverage, Oct 28, 2026)*
- **She frames "social good" as inseparable from ethics.** If NLP is to serve social good, it has to be ethical; if you want to identify and mitigate bias, you need social science perspectives on what bias is. The technical work and the social questions are one project, not two. 🟢 *(JHU faculty Q&A)*
- **She frames problems as pipeline problems.** Bias enters through data, design, and deployment, and the fix sits upstream — with the people who build the models — not with users contorting how they write. 🟡 *(inferred from the COLM 2026 paper's conclusion, which "calls for upstream consideration," and the researchers' caution against telling women to prompt differently)*
- **Her instinct when something's unfair is to measure it in the wild, then trace the mechanism.** Real prompts, real document types, four models — and then an interpretability analysis showing the linguistic features are encoded in early transformer layers, entangled with other features. 🟡 *(inferred from the paper's method: controlled experiment plus mechanistic analysis)*
- **What she'd tell a woman early in her career:** don't shrink or re-engineer how you write to please the model — the burden belongs to the people who build it, and the evidence is on your side. 🟡 *(my read from the paper's position that users "cannot easily avoid" these patterns "through strategic self-presentation")*

## 🗣️ In her words

> "If you prompt a model to write an email you're going to send to someone else at your company, and you're using language features that women more commonly use, you'll get back a response that's less complex, at a lower grade level, and less formal." (JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)

> "There's been a lot less research that really focuses on the perspective of people using these models and how models perform for different groups of people or even different individuals." (Insurance Business / MPA, Oct 28, 2026)
