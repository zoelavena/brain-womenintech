# ⚖️ Hiring and promotion bias: same experience, picked less

## ☕ The tea

Even with the same experience, women are less likely to be picked. And it's getting worse at the top: after rising from 2016 to 2022, women's share of top-level hires is falling.

## 🧾 Receipts

- **3.5:** since 2022, women's share of top-level hires has fallen more than twice as fast as their share of overall hires (−7.2% vs −3.1%). *(WEF 2026, Section 2.1, Hiring trends)*
- **Context:** women's share of top-level hires rose from 2016 to 2022, then reversed. *(same source)*
- **Pay fairness, perceived:** 58% of women said they were not compensated fairly compared with men, up from 37% in 2025; only 19% believed their pay was fair. *(Web Summit Gender in Tech 2026, community sample, via Dataconomy, Oct 30, 2026 — perceived fairness, not payroll data)*
- **Sexism at work:** 52% experienced sexism at work in the past year, vs 49% in 2025. *(same source)*
- **Pay gap, Canadian tech leadership:** women earn roughly 78 cents for every dollar men earn; median base salary $152,000 vs $194,500, a 22% gap before bonuses or equity. The gap is present at every level (Lead/Manager ~27%, C-level ~26%, Head/Director ~13%, VP ~12%, individual contributors ~20%). Bonuses: 53% of women vs 67% of men received one; medians $25,000 vs $40,000. Equity: 46% of women vs 54% of men hold it. *(Artemis Canada 2026 salary survey, self-reported, published Apr 2026; sample size not stated on the page as read)*
- **Discrimination at work:** 47% of women in tech experienced gender discrimination in the workplace, up from 42% in 2024. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026)*
- **Bias at the front door:** 30% of women say they encountered gender or diversity bias or stereotypes when entering the IT sector, vs 4% of men — and unclear career progression is the top advancement barrier for 41% of women vs 33% of men. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026; entry-bias figures via CRN Australia, Nov 2026; the 33% comparison via TNW — secondary-reported)*
- **Judged competence, AI-drafted:** in a preliminary LLM-as-judge check, AI-drafted output from male-associated prompts was picked as more competent in 69.4% of decided comparisons (67 ties; partial, single-sample). *(Van Koevering & Field, COLM 2026, Table 7)*
- **Women of colour in leadership, Canada:** representation fell from 19.7% to 14.6% year over year, the largest single-year decline recorded; women of colour hold 4% of executive-officer seats vs 13.6% of senior management roles. Sample/method not stated in the article as read. *(Prosperity Project, via Canadian HR Reporter, Oct 14, 2026)*
- **The broken rung (US/Canada):** for every 100 men promoted to manager, only 93 women were — a pattern holding for 11 consecutive years. Within entry-level roles, 30% of women vs 43% of men were promoted in the last two years. *(McKinsey & LeanIn.Org, Women in the Workplace 2025, Dec 2025; 124 organizations, 9,500 employees)*
- **Ambition gap, first time recorded:** 80% of women vs 86% of men want to be promoted to the next level. When women and men have sponsors and similar support from managers and senior colleagues, they are equally enthusiastic about promotion — the gap falls away. Entry-level women over 40: 52% want to advance vs 71% of men the same age. *(same source)*
- **Representation, European tech:** women hold 13% of management roles and 8% of senior management roles (director and C-level) in tech. 49% of women in tech experienced sexism or bias in the past year, and 82% said they must prove themselves more than men. *(McKinsey, Women in Tech and AI in Europe, Mar 5, 2026)*
- **Leaving over progression (global, all sectors):** of the 43% of women who want to leave their current job, 45% cite lack of career progression as a main reason — the top reason, ahead of earning more elsewhere (42%). *(Deloitte, Women @ Work 2026; 7,500 women, 15 countries, fieldwork May–June 2026)*
- **Pay and promotion inequity (cybersecurity):** 34% of women in cybersecurity said pay or promotion inequity prevented them moving forward, vs 19% of men; 42% of men were unaware of any significant barriers for women, vs 17% of women. *(ISC2, Women in Cybersecurity study, Mar 2026; 16,029 respondents, 2,603 women — cybersecurity only, not all of tech)*
- **Bias at the front door (Australia):** 55% of early-career respondents experienced gender bias or discrimination in job interviews, vs 42% across all career stages. Early-career women feel confident asking for a pay rise at 35% vs 61% of men — the widest gender gap on any question in the survey. The pay-perception gap (believing they are paid the same as colleagues with equivalent skills) narrowed from 18 points to 8. *(Women in Digital, Driving Change in Tech 2026, Aug 2026; 1,029 respondents, Australia)*
- **Ambition is belief-contingent (US, Gen Z, all sectors):** 58% of Gen Z women vs 74% of Gen Z men want a senior executive role someday — but 74% of Gen Z women who believe workplaces genuinely want women in leadership want the top job, matching men. Only 43% of Gen Z women hold that belief (vs 56% of men). *(LeanIn.Org, Gen Z Women and Leadership factsheet, Apr 2026; survey of 3,007 US adults, May 5–19, 2026, Gen Z ages 18–29 — general workforce, not tech-only)*
- **Burnout, same survey as the discrimination receipt:** 64% of women report work-related stress or burnout, vs 57% of men; heavy workload (48%), lack of resources (43%), and unsupportive management (42%) are the top drivers. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026; via ISACA's own press release, Oct 13, 2026)*
- **Pay gap, European tech (pay ruler):** 20% unadjusted, 1% adjusted in 2026 — unadjusted unchanged from 2025. Engineering is the widest function (21% unadjusted, 5% adjusted; women 19% of Engineering). The unadjusted gap peaks at the step into management (M2, 15%) and narrows to 3% at executive level, where women hold 22% of roles but earn 3–8% less base, 21–28% less bonus, and 18–27% less annual equity than male peers. Women are 36% of the European tech workforce and 25% of the highest-paid quarter. *(Ravio, Compensation Trends 2027, page updated Nov 4, 2026; article body cites 1,600+ European tech companies, source line cites 1,800+ companies and 618,000+ datapoints — discrepancy noted in the radar)*
- **Equal Pay Day moved backwards (UK, all sectors — not tech-specific):** the mean full-time gender pay gap rose from 10.9% to 11.5%, so Equal Pay Day 2026 falls on Nov 20, two days earlier than last year, and women effectively work without pay for the final 42 days of the year. Mean full-time hourly pay excluding overtime: £26.90 (men) vs £23.81 (women). Equal Pay Day arrived Nov 20, 2026; on Nov 6 the UK government announced salary information would be mandatory in job adverts, and Fawcett called for a ban on salary-history questions and a legal Right to Know, warning AI systems can reproduce pay inequalities when they use historical salary data to recommend starting pay. *(Fawcett Society, Nov 2026; ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings, provisional)*
- **Representation is falling, not flat (European tech):** women are 36% of the European tech workforce in 2026, down from 38% in 2025 and 41% in 2024. Women hold 22% of executive roles, 36% of management roles, 40% of professional (IC) roles; Engineering is lowest at 19% (flat across all three years), and Sales fell 42% → 33% in a year. The UK holds steady at 41%; Germany and the Netherlands are lowest at 34%. *(Ravio, Compensation Trends 2027; blog "Women in tech statistics 2026", page updated Nov 4, 2026; live data from 1,800+ companies, 618,000+ datapoints)*
- **Promotion takes twice as long (UK):** men are promoted in two years; women in four. Pay disparities reach up to £50,000, and promotion delays sit "at every single juncture of a woman's career." *(Lovelace Report, WeAreTechWomen & Oliver Wyman, 2025; 500+ mid-to-senior women surveyed, 20 senior technologists interviewed, Mercer Comptryx data from 600+ UK tech firms — as reported by Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*
- **The mid-career exit, counted (UK):** 55% of women leave tech roles or tech companies within 5 years of entering the industry, and nearly 9 in 10 (87%) within 10 years. Top reasons: lack of a sense of belonging (52%) and lack of gender diversity in leadership (40%); inflexible hours (56%) and lack of work-life balance (42%) among those who left. Only 15% left the job market — the rest moved to finance (13%), education (13%), professional services (12%) and healthcare (12%). 39% of women who left would consider returning under the right conditions — higher salary (52%), work-life balance (43%) and better career progression (43%). *(Akamai, commissioned research by Censuswide, Apr 13, 2026; fieldwork March 2026; 1,500 UK women — 1,000 who left tech, 500 who returned after a career break)*
- **The deadline that passed unmet (Austria):** the June 7, 2026 deadline for transposing the EU Pay Transparency Directive came and went without Austria transposing it. A draft bill would require employers with 100+ staff to file regular income reports, with fines up to €60,000 for repeat violations — but the governing coalition has yet to agree on it, and Austria faces possible EU infringement proceedings. Austria's national pay gap stands at 15.5% (down from 16.3%), with women effectively unpaid from Nov 5, 2026. *(ad-hoc-news, Nov 1, 2026, citing the Chamber of Labour Upper Austria and Statistics Austria wage-tax data for 2024)*
- **Ambition, redefined:** 83% of women in tech aspire to executive leadership, but 43% are increasingly content in their current positions; among those not pursuing executive leadership, preference for hands-on technical work rose to 41% from 35% last year. Work-life balance is the most important workplace factor (40%) and a top reason for not pursuing leadership (33%). *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, Nov 5, 2026; 1,500 female-identifying tech professionals, US/UK/India)*
- **The authority gap (tech, 2026):** women are less likely than men to feel sufficient authority over company strategy (53% vs 66%), personnel decisions (48% vs 59%), and purchasing decisions (32% vs 52% — the widest split, 20 points). Satisfaction with workplace culture runs 52% vs 65% and job security 59% vs 69% — while progression itself is at parity: 73% of women vs 71% of men got a salary increase or promotion in the past two years. The promotion happened; the decision rights didn't follow. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026; figures verified at ISACA's own blog and infographic, Nov 2026 — closing the Nov 4 gap, when the culture-satisfaction numbers were secondary-only. The survey measures perceived authority, not formal approval rules, and does not claim the gap drives the satisfaction differences.)*
- **Management pipeline thinning below the top (global enterprises):** women hold 30.5% of managerial roles worldwide. The share of enterprises with a woman board chair rose from 13.1% (2013) to 30.9% (2025) and with a woman CEO from 23.4% to 28.2% — but gender balance declined at junior and middle management over the same period, where future leaders are trained. *(ILO, Women in Business and Management: Scaling Success, Nov 3, 2026; 2025 survey of 11,957 enterprises in 99 countries, via UN News. Note: UN News reported the board-chair figure as 30.1%; the World Research Institute's analysis of the ILO report quotes 30.9%, settling it in favour of the ILO's own figure.)*
- **The drop to the top, measured at the hire:** women's share of new hires into top-level management has fallen every year since 2022, from 34.8% to 32.3% in the first half of 2026, and their share of C-suite hires has stalled around 27% since 2022. Women hold 8.6% of CTO roles, 19% of CEO roles overall, and 6.8% of Fortune Global 500 CEO roles (a record 34 women). *(WEF, Global Gender Gap Report 2026, Sept 16, 2026, and LinkedIn Economic Graph data, as reported by Slayton Search, Nov 4, 2026 — secondary-reported; the WEF report itself was not opened.)*
- **Automation exposure and biased recruiting (AI harms, labour cluster):** 15 of the 20 occupations most exposed to automation are female-dominated; the snapshot names biased recruiting systems that suppress wages and amplify pay gaps, alongside female-dominated occupations disproportionately automated and reinforced occupational segregation. Filed as a mechanism claim, not a payroll figure. New from the snapshot PDF: a 2025 Australian study testing OpenAI and Microsoft models as preferred job candidates in software engineering found the models favoured male and Caucasian profiles, especially in senior roles; the Australian tech sector's total remuneration gender pay gap runs significantly higher than the economy-wide average, and nearly half of women in STEM report harassment at five times the rate reported by men; and the "ghost work" behind AI — violent-content data-labelling workers, often outsourced to the Global South — is traumatising and low-paid. *(Victorian Women's Trust, Gender and AI: Where's the Harm?, Oct 2026; automation figure via Jobs and Skills Australia)*
- **The Triple Penalty in AI hiring (US + 27 countries):** women were 26% of U.S. hires into AI jobs in 2025 vs 50% into non-AI jobs; 20% of Head of AI hires, 26% of Director of AI, 18% of Member of Technical Staff; 29% of entry-level AI roles at AI companies; 13% of C-suite AI roles at AI companies (31.2% of AI leaders at Director+ vs 38.5% of leaders overall). The three penalties, estimated after accounting for other factors: C-suite −15.3 points, AI role −9.7, AI company −5.2. Typical AI posting lists ~$177,000 vs ~$80,000 for non-AI roles. *(LinkedIn Economic Graph Research Institute, The Triple Penalty, Aug 18, 2026 — secondary-reported via Fortune, Media Week, diginomica, WomenTech Network; the LinkedIn report itself was not opened)*
- **Men preferred in AI — the workers say so (Sweden):** 59.7% of women and non-binary professionals in Sweden's AI industry say men are preferred in hiring, promotion, or influence in AI; among technical/coding respondents, 51.2% say men are preferred, with bigger challenges being taken seriously, getting project ownership, and receiving recognition. *(Women in AI Sweden Report 2026; n=114, self-selected community survey — a community's experience, not a workforce measure)*
- **The pay gap employers can see — and do nothing about (US):** 73% of employers believe pay is equal at their organisation vs 50% of employees; 69% of men believe pay is equal at their own company vs 43% of women. 27% of women say they personally earn less than a colleague of the opposite gender in the same role, vs 3% of men — and 37% of primary caregivers vs 25% of other women. 49% of employers conduct no pay equity review at all; among those that do, 30% found a gap and made no change, and only 16% ever adjusted anyone's pay after a review found a discrepancy. Executives are 3x more optimistic than employees that AI will help close the pay gap (33% vs 11%), even as 25% of employers already use AI or algorithmic tools to help set salaries. *(Omni Calculator, Gender Pay Gap Perception Survey 2026, Nov 9, 2026; two parallel surveys, 808 employed US adults and 403 founders/CEOs/C-suite executives — a commercial survey; perceived pay fairness is labelled as perceived throughout)
- **Bias in the promotion decision, admitted by managers (UK, all sectors):** four in five managers can't rule out gender bias affecting promotion decisions; only 17% say stereotypes or unconscious bias never affect them. 24% cite a male-dominated culture or bias as the main barrier to young women securing their first management or leadership role. Only 34% strongly agree their organisation provides equal progression opportunities — 24% of female managers vs 44% of male managers. *(CMI, Ready to Lead, Oct 2026; 601 UK managers, with Girlguiding, Young Women's Trust and Women@Tech — general workforce, not tech-only)*
- **Agency up, visibility flat (developers):** 64% of women developers say AI improved their ability to shape their career direction and 84% report stronger career momentum linked to AI skills — but only 33% say AI adoption increased their visibility or access to opportunities. Confidence in the gender fairness of AI-supported tools (performance reviews, task allocation) runs 56% among women vs 64% among men. *(BairesDev, Dev Barometer Q1 2026; 1,329 developers across 61 countries, surveyed January 2026, 25% women — vendor survey)*
- **The pay-quartile ladder (European tech):** women are 48% of the lowest-paid quarter, 40% of the second, 30% of the third and 25% of the highest-paid quarter. Country splits: UK 51% → 26%, Germany 45% → 22%, Netherlands 50% → 25%, France 49% → 26%, Spain 51% → 28%, Sweden 48% → 28% — Sweden breaks the pattern, its top quartile more balanced than its third. *(Ravio, Compensation Trends 2027; blog "Women in tech statistics 2026", updated Nov 4, 2026; live data from 1,800+ companies, 618,000+ datapoints)*
- **The hidden penalty of using AI at work (engineers):** in a pre-registered experiment with 1,026 engineers at one large tech company, reviewers rated identical Python code 9% lower on competence when they believed AI had assisted — 13% for women engineers vs 6% for men. Male engineers who don't use AI themselves penalised female colleagues who used it 26% more harshly than they penalised men for identical AI use. A follow-up survey of 919 engineers found women and older engineers anticipated the penalty and avoided AI to protect their reputations; adoption at the company was just 41% overall, 31% among women. The cost: a net loss of at least 2.5% of annual profit, potentially as high as 14%. *(Harvard Business Review, Aug 1, 2025; 28,698 engineers studied overall)*
- **The praise gap and the cheating worry:** women are 32% more likely than men to worry they'll be perceived as cheating when they use AI — and men who used AI at work were 27% more likely to have been praised for doing so than women. Deloitte: 93% of men vs 84% of women say their company actively encourages AI use, and 91% of men vs 72% of women have received formal AI training. Women are almost twice as likely as men to predict more women than men will be laid off due to AI. *(LeanIn.Org, November 2026, synthesis of its March 2026 survey of 1,000+ Americans and Deloitte research)*
- **The glass cliff, closed in eleven days (Nvidia):** the board unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board director on November 23, 2026 — eleven days after his November 12 resignation, and six days after the November 17 earnings call ruled out his return. CFO Colette Kress, interim CEO for those eleven days, returns to the CFO job; the Heidrick & Struggles search never got to run. The board had found Huang's behaviour breached its code of conduct but said it was satisfied with his candour; shares surged 27% on the day. On NPR, [[freada-kapor-klein|Freada Kapor Klein]] called it a resounding message that all that matters is money, and former Uber chief diversity officer Bo Young Lee's first word was "unfathomable" — NPR reached out to two dozen women in tech and few would talk on the record. The independent investigation continues. *(NPR, All Things Considered, Nov 23, 2026; TechCrunch / CoinDesk, Nov 23, 2026)*
- **Nvidia, the case stays open:** the board restored Huang before its own investigation concluded — the committee of independent directors is still examining the reports, with independent counsel — and added governance around the return: a new National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors, and a search for a chief compliance officer. The tests now have dates: a closed-door transcribed interview with three House committees on Dec 1 (general counsel Tim Teter on Dec 2) and a public Senate Banking Committee hearing on Dec 3. Beyond Congress, Reuters reported Nov 20 that SEC enforcement staff are scrutinising potential accounting issues (early stage, could end without charges), the Wall Street Journal reported a civil probe into Nvidia's disclosures on Nov 17, California's attorney general served an investigative subpoena Nov 19, and a securities class action was filed Nov 9 (lead-plaintiff deadline Jan 8, 2027). *(Bloomberg Law, Nov 23, 2026; Reuters, Nov 17–20, 2026)*
- **One pay gap, two shapes (developers, global):** women developers earn 17.6% less than men after adjusting for human capital, technical skills and firm characteristics. The gap splits by income level: 8.8% in high-income economies, 22.9% upper-middle, 36.6% lower-middle — and 47.9% in South Asia. In high-income countries the gap widens toward the top (glass ceiling: top-decile coefficient −0.1968 unconditional); in lower-middle-income countries it is worst at the bottom (sticky floor: −0.4444 at the first decile). The unexplained share runs 45% in high-income countries and 68.5% elsewhere (pooled Oaxaca-Blinder). Women stay concentrated in QA, testing and client-facing roles rather than systems engineering or executive leadership. *(Prakash & Yadav, Journal for Labour Market Research 59(1), Article 17, 2025; 53,507 developers, 173 countries, 2022 Stack Overflow Developer Survey — payroll data, self-reported, binary-gendered)*
- **Ambition falls with support, not with caring (entry level):** 88% of entry-level women say their career is important to them, roughly the same as entry-level men, but only 69% say they want a promotion, vs 80% of men — and almost 25% of entry- and senior-level women who aren't interested in promotion say personal obligations like housework make extra work hard, vs 15% of comparable men. *(McKinsey & Company, "Mind the Gap," Jan 6, 2026, reading Women in the Workplace 2025)*
- **The opt-out isn't confidence:** among women not pursuing executive leadership, only 11% cite a lack of confidence and just 6% point to a skills gap. The opt-out is satisfaction (43%) and a preference for hands-on technical work (41%, up from 35%). *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, Nov 5, 2026; 1,500 female-identifying tech professionals, US/UK/India — enrichment of the Nov 5 filing)*
- **The AI hiring audit — the average says no bias, the occupations say otherwise:** in a preregistered audit of six language models across 98 US occupations (35,280 CVs generated, gender randomly assigned), the average screening gap is close to zero and not statistically significant — but in occupations where women are under 10% of the workforce, women score about 2.7% lower (1.6 points evaluation bias, 1.1 generation bias) and are 4.8 percentage points less likely to pass the initial screen; where women are over 80%, they gain a similar advantage. The CVs written for women lean on helping others and non-promotable tasks, and a word-count classifier still recovers the intended gender 81% of the time with names and pronouns stripped. Salary recommendations for explicitly identified women run about 1.6% lower, with bigger penalties where the observed pay gap is wider. Asking the model to be gender-neutral leaves the biases largely intact. Lab simulations in English, US context — not real hiring outcomes. *(Elass, Gauthier, Profeta & Nozza, PNAS, Nov 16, 2026; lead author [[kenza-elass\|Kenza Elass]] seated at the Tea Party as brewing, researcher)*
- **Equal Pay Day, and the law that would enforce it is late (Netherlands):** women earn 9.8% less per hour than men on average (CBS, final 2025 figures, published Oct 16, 2026), and Women Inc. calculates a woman loses around €300,000 over her working life while the gap persists; the unexplained gap runs 6.1% in the private sector and 11.6% in financial institutions. The bill transposing the EU Pay Transparency Directive — salary-history ban, mandatory pay-gap reporting for employers over 100 staff, retroactive claims up to five years, action plans where an unjustified gap exceeds 5% — was meant to take effect Jan 1, 2027, but the House will not debate it until Dec 14 and the Senate's final meeting of the year is Dec 15, so it will likely miss its start. *(Atria, Nov 16, 2026; Dutch Times, Nov 19, 2026)*
- **The office brings back the office housework:** a Rotman School of Management study of 1,091 professional women in the US found 31% reported everyday gender discrimination when working on-site, compared with 17% when working remotely — including being assigned tasks that weren't their responsibility. In the UK, a Co-operatives UK survey of 2,000 working women and men found 22% of women had been asked to do paperwork outside their role, 21% to clean or tidy the office, and 20% to make coffee for senior leaders; nearly half (49%) of women said they'd been asked more than once to do something outside their role. The non-promotable work now has a location: the office itself. *(Fast Company, Nov 20, 2026)*
- **Nvidia, the tests survived the reinstatement:** the chairs of the three House committees confirmed on Nov 23 that Huang and general counsel Tim Teter will still appear for transcribed interviews on Dec 1 and Dec 2 as previously planned — the board's internal investigation and reinstatement "do not erase the national security threats and questions that must be addressed." The Senate Banking Committee hearing is titled "Protecting America's AI Advantage: Oversight of U.S. Export Controls," Dec 3 at 10 a.m. ET, with Huang as a witness and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asked to testify Dec 10. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer alleged on Nov 24 that Huang violated federal export restrictions on advanced chips to China, and CBS reported Nov 24 that Huang continues to hold a security clearance. *(AIP, Nov 24, 2026; KLZ / AP, Nov 24–25, 2026)*

## 🗣️ Real talk

**[[anjalie-field|Anjalie Field]]** on what an AI-drafted email does to how a woman is seen at work: the weaker draft lands before any hiring decision is made. "That's going to reflect on how the recipient of that document perceives you." *(JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*

**[[vanessa-vallely|Vanessa Vallely]]** on the promotion clock: men get promoted in two years; for women it's four — and on the fix: "It is not about fixing women. We don't need fixing. It's the systems which we operate in that do." *(Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*

## 💔 Why it matters

The reversal after 2022 means progress isn't a ramp. It can be undone, and quietly.

## 💭 Still brewing

- Nov 22: the competence penalty is now measured in a working engineering org (HBR, Aug 2025) — women engineers who use AI are rated 13% less competent for identical code, vs 6% for men, and the harshest judges are male non-adopters. Open: does the penalty show up in promotion data, or only in peer ratings? And does the LeanIn praise gap (men 27% more likely to be praised for AI use) mean the same mechanism is already shaping who gets seen as promotable?
- Nov 23: Nvidia — the glass cliff closed in eleven days. The board ruled out Huang's return on Nov 17 and reinstated him unanimously on Nov 23; the Heidrick & Struggles search never ran, and Kress is back to CFO. The independent investigation continues — does it still matter now that he's back, or is the case closed with the reinstatement? And NPR's reporting adds a second signal: of two dozen women in tech approached, few would talk on the record, and the conversations are quieter than in 2017–18. The rooms under strain, made audible.
- Nov 24: the case stays open, and the question answers itself. The board restored Huang *before* its own investigation concluded — it reached its conclusion about his candour ahead of the investigators it appointed to test it — and the tests now have dates: Dec 1 (House, closed-door), Dec 3 (Senate Banking, public), plus an SEC accounting probe, a California subpoena and a class action. The new governance (a National Security and Export Control Committee, a chief compliance officer search) is the first institutional answer to Kapor Klein's guardrails line — guardrails for CEOs, not just for the tools — but it arrives after the verdict, not before it. Open: does the case close when the investigation reports, when Congress hears him, or when neither moves anything?
- ISACA's culture-satisfaction split (52% of women vs 65% of men) and the full authority gap (purchasing 32% vs 52%, personnel 48% vs 59%) are now filed as receipts (Nov 13) — verified at ISACA's own blog and infographic, closing the Nov 4 gap. The open question sharpens: progression is at parity (73% vs 71% promoted or raised) while decision rights lag 13–20 points. Is authority the promotion that doesn't come with the title?
- The Artemis pay gap by level isn't a staircase: ~20% for individual contributors, ~27% Lead/Manager, ~13% Head/Director, ~12% VP, ~26% C-level. It narrows mid-career and reopens at the top. Real, or small samples per level? Artemis states no sample size, and I have no source on why. It's also a pay ruler, separate from the representation numbers above. *(see [[what-shes-learning]], 2026-11-03)*
- What happened in 2022? Layoffs, cuts to DEI budgets, the return-to-office push? We need sources before we claim anything.
- Pay-fairness perception worsened sharply (37% → 58%). Is that the actual gap widening, transparency making it visible, or both? We need payroll data, not just perceptions, before we call it.
- Artemis narrows that question without closing it: we now have pay medians, not just feelings — but they are self-reported survey medians, not a payroll audit, and the sample size isn't stated. Still brewing: how much of the gap is counting, and how much is the gap itself?
- ISACA: discrimination reports rose (42% → 47%) while awareness of the issue also rose. Naming the problem isn't turning into change — what turns awareness into action? The Omni survey (Nov 17) sharpens it into a number: 30% of employers *found* a pay gap and made no change, and 49% never look at all. So the question is no longer whether companies know — it's what makes a company act on what it already knows.
- The Prosperity Project decline names the intersection the other receipts average over: promotion at the top runs on "who you know... and how an individual is seen as the ideal leader" (Julie Savard-Shaw), and women of colour "simply aren't being tapped on the shoulder." The sponsorship gap (4.5) needs that intersection, not just the gender average.
- Nov 21: two representation rulers moved the wrong way at once — Grant Thornton's mid-market senior-management share fell 34.0% → 32.9% (parity not until 2051), and cybersecurity startups are flat at 24.5% after five years. Open: is the 32.9% a bounce-back dip, as Grant Thornton says past dips were, or the ILO's thinning pipeline showing up at the top? And the GenAI double bind: using the tool that proves her competence costs her warmth, especially in research. Open: does the warmth penalty show up in promotion decisions, or only in vignette ratings?
