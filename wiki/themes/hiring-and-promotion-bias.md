# ⚖️ Hiring and promotion bias: same experience, picked less

## ☕ The tea

Even with the same experience, women are less likely to be picked. And it's getting worse at the top: after rising from 2016 to 2022, women's share of top-level hires is falling.

## 🧾 Receipts

- **3.5:** since 2022, women's share of top-level hires has fallen more than twice as fast as their share of overall hires (−7.2% vs −3.1%). *(WEF 2026, Section 2.1, Hiring trends)*
- **Context:** women's share of top-level hires rose from 2016 to 2022, then reversed. *(same source)*
- **Pay fairness, perceived:** 58% of women said they were not compensated fairly compared with men, up from 37% in 2025; only 19% believed their pay was fair. *(Web Summit Gender in Tech 2026, community sample, via Dataconomy, Oct 30, 2026 — perceived fairness, not payroll data)*
- **Sexism at work:** 52% experienced sexism at work in the past year, vs 49% in 2025. *(same source)*
- **Pay gap, Canadian tech leadership:** women earn roughly 78 cents for every dollar men earn; median base salary $152,000 vs $194,500, a 22% gap before bonuses or equity. The gap is present at every level (Lead/Manager ~27%, C-level ~26%, Head/Director ~13%, VP ~12%, individual contributors ~20%). Bonuses: 53% of women vs 67% of men received one; medians $25,000 vs $40,000. Equity: 46% of women vs 54% of men hold it. *(Artemis Canada 2026 salary survey, self-reported, published Apr 2026; sample size not stated on the page as read)*
- **Discrimination at work:** 47% of women in tech experienced gender discrimination in the workplace, up from 42% in 2024. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026)*
- **Judged competence, AI-drafted:** in a preliminary LLM-as-judge check, AI-drafted output from male-associated prompts was picked as more competent in 69.4% of decided comparisons (67 ties; partial, single-sample). *(Van Koevering & Field, COLM 2026, Table 7)*
- **Women of colour in leadership, Canada:** representation fell from 19.7% to 14.6% year over year, the largest single-year decline recorded; women of colour hold 4% of executive-officer seats vs 13.6% of senior management roles. Sample/method not stated in the article as read. *(Prosperity Project, via Canadian HR Reporter, Oct 14, 2026)*
- **The broken rung (US/Canada):** for every 100 men promoted to manager, only 93 women were — a pattern holding for 11 consecutive years. Within entry-level roles, 30% of women vs 43% of men were promoted in the last two years. *(McKinsey & LeanIn.Org, Women in the Workplace 2025, Dec 2025; 124 organizations, 9,500 employees)*
- **Ambition gap, first time recorded:** 80% of women vs 86% of men want to be promoted to the next level. When women and men have sponsors and similar support from managers and senior colleagues, they are equally enthusiastic about promotion — the gap falls away. Entry-level women over 40: 52% want to advance vs 71% of men the same age. *(same source)*
- **Representation, European tech:** women hold 13% of management roles and 8% of senior management roles (director and C-level) in tech. 49% of women in tech experienced sexism or bias in the past year, and 82% said they must prove themselves more than men. *(McKinsey, Women in Tech and AI in Europe, Mar 5, 2026)*
- **Leaving over progression (global, all sectors):** of the 43% of women who want to leave their current job, 45% cite lack of career progression as a main reason — the top reason, ahead of earning more elsewhere (42%). *(Deloitte, Women @ Work 2026; 7,500 women, 15 countries, fieldwork May–June 2026)*
- **Pay and promotion inequity (cybersecurity):** 34% of women in cybersecurity said pay or promotion inequity prevented them moving forward, vs 19% of men; 42% of men were unaware of any significant barriers for women, vs 17% of women. *(ISC2, Women in Cybersecurity study, Mar 2026; 16,029 respondents, 2,603 women — cybersecurity only, not all of tech)*
- **Bias at the front door (Australia):** 55% of early-career respondents experienced gender bias or discrimination in job interviews, vs 42% across all career stages. Early-career women feel confident asking for a pay rise at 35% vs 61% of men — the widest gender gap on any question in the survey. The pay-perception gap (believing they are paid the same as colleagues with equivalent skills) narrowed from 18 points to 8. *(Women in Digital, Driving Change in Tech 2026, Aug 2026; 1,029 respondents, Australia)*
- **Ambition is belief-contingent (US, Gen Z, all sectors):** 58% of Gen Z women vs 74% of Gen Z men want a senior executive role someday — but 74% of Gen Z women who believe workplaces genuinely want women in leadership want the top job, matching men. Only 43% of Gen Z women hold that belief (vs 56% of men). *(LeanIn.Org, Gen Z Women and Leadership factsheet, Apr 2026; survey of 3,007 US adults, May 5–19, 2026, Gen Z ages 18–29 — general workforce, not tech-only)*
- **Burnout, same survey as the discrimination receipt:** 64% of women report work-related stress or burnout, vs 57% of men; heavy workload (48%), lack of resources (43%), and unsupportive management (42%) are the top drivers. *(ISACA 2026 Tech Workplace and Culture survey, 1,025 global tech respondents, fieldwork Jul 17–Aug 3, 2026; via ISACA's own press release, Oct 13, 2026)*
- **Pay gap, European tech (pay ruler):** 20% unadjusted, 1% adjusted in 2026 — unadjusted unchanged from 2025. Engineering is the widest function (21% unadjusted, 5% adjusted; women 19% of Engineering). The unadjusted gap peaks at the step into management (M2, 15%) and narrows to 3% at executive level, where women hold 22% of roles but earn 3–8% less base, 21–28% less bonus, and 18–27% less annual equity than male peers. Women are 36% of the European tech workforce and 25% of the highest-paid quarter. *(Ravio, Compensation Trends 2027, page updated Nov 4, 2026; article body cites 1,600+ European tech companies, source line cites 1,800+ companies and 618,000+ datapoints — discrepancy noted in the radar)*
- **Ambition, redefined:** 83% of women in tech aspire to executive leadership, but 43% are increasingly content in their current positions; among those not pursuing executive leadership, preference for hands-on technical work rose to 41% from 35% last year. Work-life balance is the most important workplace factor (40%) and a top reason for not pursuing leadership (33%). *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, Nov 5, 2026; 1,500 female-identifying tech professionals, US/UK/India)*
- **Automation exposure and biased recruiting (AI harms, labour cluster):** 15 of the 20 occupations most exposed to automation are female-dominated; the snapshot names biased recruiting systems that suppress wages and amplify pay gaps, alongside female-dominated occupations disproportionately automated and reinforced occupational segregation. Filed as a mechanism claim, not a payroll figure. *(Victorian Women's Trust, Gender and AI: Where's the Harm?, Oct 2026; automation figure via Jobs and Skills Australia)*

## 🗣️ Real talk

**[[anjalie-field|Anjalie Field]]** on what an AI-drafted email does to how a woman is seen at work: the weaker draft lands before any hiring decision is made. "That's going to reflect on how the recipient of that document perceives you." *(JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*

## 💔 Why it matters

The reversal after 2022 means progress isn't a ramp. It can be undone, and quietly.

## 💭 Still brewing

- ISACA's culture-satisfaction split (52% of women vs 65% of men satisfied with workplace culture) appeared in secondary coverage of the survey, but not in ISACA's own press release or the report-hub text I could read — so it's not filed as a receipt. File it if the full report is obtained. The direction is confirmed at primary (women report lower satisfaction with culture than men); the numbers aren't.
- The Artemis pay gap by level isn't a staircase: ~20% for individual contributors, ~27% Lead/Manager, ~13% Head/Director, ~12% VP, ~26% C-level. It narrows mid-career and reopens at the top. Real, or small samples per level? Artemis states no sample size, and I have no source on why. It's also a pay ruler, separate from the representation numbers above. *(see [[what-shes-learning]], 2026-11-03)*
- What happened in 2022? Layoffs, cuts to DEI budgets, the return-to-office push? We need sources before we claim anything.
- Pay-fairness perception worsened sharply (37% → 58%). Is that the actual gap widening, transparency making it visible, or both? We need payroll data, not just perceptions, before we call it.
- Artemis narrows that question without closing it: we now have pay medians, not just feelings — but they are self-reported survey medians, not a payroll audit, and the sample size isn't stated. Still brewing: how much of the gap is counting, and how much is the gap itself?
- ISACA: discrimination reports rose (42% → 47%) while awareness of the issue also rose. Naming the problem isn't turning into change — what turns awareness into action?
- The Prosperity Project decline names the intersection the other receipts average over: promotion at the top runs on "who you know... and how an individual is seen as the ideal leader" (Julie Savard-Shaw), and women of colour "simply aren't being tapped on the shoulder." The sponsorship gap (4.5) needs that intersection, not just the gender average.
