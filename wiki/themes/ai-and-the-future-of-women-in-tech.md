# 🤖 AI and the future of women in tech

## ☕ The tea

Girl, every new wave of tech is a fresh chance to get it right, and the early receipts say we're repeating the old pattern. When AI roles open up, women land in the lower-paid ones. It grew out of node 3.1 on the original tree, and it's a theme of its own because AI is reshaping entry-level work, promotion paths, and who gets to build the tools.

## 🧾 Receipts

- **3.1, who gets which AI job:** women hold 45% of data annotator roles and 19% of AI engineer roles. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- Data annotators earn less than the median non-AI job. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- **AI job growth went almost entirely to men (US):** for each 1-point rise in an occupation's AI-exposure score (0–3.5, 480 occupations), men's employment grew 6.3% vs 0.2% for women (2017–19 vs Jan 2023–June 2026). Widest without a bachelor's degree: high school or less, +8.8% men vs −9.5% women; bachelor's+, 0.8% vs 0.7%, neither significant. Pew cautions the link is not necessarily causal. *(Pew Research Center, Oct 22, 2026; CPS + O*NET)*
- **The access gap closed; the confidence gap didn't (US):** ever-use of AI chatbots is now 50% of men vs 47% of women (from 39% vs 28% in 2024), but men use them daily more (27% vs 20%), for work more (40% vs 35%), say they help productivity more (35% vs 25%), and feel extremely/very confident more (22% vs 15%). Women are about twice as likely to say AI will affect them personally negatively rather than positively over 20 years (33% vs 17%). *(Pew Research Center, The Gender Gap in AI, Oct 16, 2026; survey Feb 17–23, 2026)*
- **Left out of AI, on every dimension (US):** women more likely than men to have never used AI (29% vs 22%), less likely to use it regularly (16% vs 25%); 46% of women never use AI at work vs 36% of men; 64% of women report receiving *no* AI training at work vs 49% of men, with less mentorship and encouragement; men named in AI headlines 14.6 times as often as women, cited as experts 4.3 times as often (women 9.4% of authority citations). Support — training, mentorship, encouragement — is associated with greater use, confidence, and perceived value for both women and men. *(Harvard Shorenstein Center, Are Women Being Left Out of AI?, Oct 14, 2026; CHIP50 survey, 5,403 U.S. adults, July 2026; 78,542 news articles)*
- **The adoption gap is a training gap (Denmark):** women were 16 percentage points less likely than men in the same occupation to have used ChatGPT for work (12 points within the same workplace, controlling for task mix). Among workers who saw large time savings but didn't intend to use it, 45% of women cited a need for training; men more often cited employer restrictions and confidentiality. *(Humlum & Vestergaard, PNAS 122(1), e2414972121; 18,000 workers, 11 occupations — secondary-reported via Medium, Nov 6, 2026)*
- **The support fix, measured (Denmark):** employer encouragement cut the gender gap in ever-use of ChatGPT from 11.9 to 5 percentage points; employer-provided training cut it to 3.6 points. *(Humlum & Vestergaard, BFI Working Paper 2025–56 — secondary-reported via Medium, Nov 6, 2026; the working paper itself was not opened)*
- **The confidence paradox (Singapore):** 84% of young women use AI tools at least once a week (37% daily), 75% show a core foundational understanding, and fewer hold misconceptions than men (25% vs 34%) — yet only 6% feel very confident they will thrive in an AI-driven environment vs 16% of men, only 8% see themselves as AI leaders or guides vs 20% of men, and only 21% believe AI careers are equally open to both genders vs 50% of men. *(United Women Singapore, with the Micron Foundation; Milieu Insight, May–June 2026; 800 students and young career starters aged 16–29, 640 women, 160 men — secondary-reported via FutureCIO, Nov 6, 2026)*
- **The gap is small — and flat — in Ireland:** 29.0% of men and 27.8% of women intentionally use GenAI at work; adjusted for education, occupation, sector, firm size, contract type and age, women are about 4 points less likely to use it (−3.6, p ≈ 0.05). The gap does not widen with intensity: frequency, multi-tool use and depth of engagement run along occupational and educational lines, not gender — unlike the US evidence. Only 42.8% of users in the Republic and 37.7% in Northern Ireland have received or are receiving employer training, and the productivity gains "are flowing predominantly to employers and not to workers." Use is steeply stratified by earnings: 8.5% of workers earning under €15,000 vs 73.8% of those earning €110,000+. *(Working in Ireland Survey, GenAI Use in Work, Nov 2026; nationally representative sample of 4,300 workers)*
- **The Triple Penalty, decomposed:** women were 26% of U.S. hires into AI jobs in 2025 vs 50% into non-AI jobs; 20% of Head of AI hires, 26% of Director of AI, 18% of Member of Technical Staff; 29% of entry-level AI roles at AI companies; 13% of C-suite AI roles at AI companies across 27 countries (31.2% of AI leaders at Director+ vs 38.5% of leaders overall). Typical AI posting lists ~$177,000 vs ~$80,000 for non-AI roles. *(LinkedIn Economic Graph Research Institute, The Triple Penalty, Aug 18, 2026 — secondary-reported; upgrades the Nov 2 receipt, which carried the 26%/50% and 13% figures via TechTimes)*
- **6 in 10 say men are preferred in AI (Sweden):** in a community survey of 114 women and non-binary professionals in Sweden's AI industry, 59.7% say men are preferred in hiring, promotion, or influence in AI; among technical/coding respondents, 51.2% say men are preferred, with bigger challenges being taken seriously, getting project ownership, and receiving recognition. *(Women in AI Sweden Report 2026; n=114, self-selected community survey — a community's experience, not a workforce measure)*
- **Hiring flow, 2025:** women were 26% of new AI hires in the US vs 50% of new non-AI hires, and held 13% of C-suite AI roles across 27 countries. *(LinkedIn research, via TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*
- **Perceived impact:** 31% of women said AI had improved gender equality, 19% said it had made it worse, 34% saw no impact, 16% unsure. *(Web Summit Gender in Tech 2026, 1,000+ women in Web Summit's community — not a representative sample of the whole workforce)*
- **Prompt register:** prompts carrying women-associated linguistic features (hedges, tag questions, collective reference, expressive adjectives) elicited shorter, less sophisticated, less formal responses across three document types and four models (GPT-4, Llama, Gemma, Mistral); effects persisted after controlling for prompt complexity and style carry-over, and a male sign-off name had virtually no effect. *(Van Koevering & Field, Johns Hopkins, "It's How You Ask: Gender-Associated Linguistic Bias in LLMs", COLM 2026; JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*
- **LLM-as-judge, preliminary:** in a partial GPT-4-only judge run (105 comparisons), the male-associated prompt output was chosen as more competent in 69.4% of decided comparisons and smarter in 68.3%; the judge tied most of the time (67 ties on competence). The authors call the analysis partial and single-sample. *(same paper, Table 7)*
- **Automation risk, uneven:** 21% of women's employment is in high-automation-risk occupations vs 10% of men's; 9% of women's jobs vs 13% of men's are projected to grow with AI. Scenario analysis grounded in the Richmond AI Jobs Transition Framework, not predictions; the authors caution the framework may overstate substitution risk. *(Philadelphia Fed, "Which Workers Are Most Vulnerable to AI Disruption?", circulated Oct 30, 2026; BLS OEWS + ACS PUMS data)*
- **AI skills up, workload up:** 70% of women in tech rate their generative AI knowledge and skills as advanced or expert (experts doubled since 2024), yet 42% say they are expected to produce more work in less time because of AI; 87% report at least some level of burnout, 60% moderate, very or extreme. *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, Nov 5, 2026; 1,500 female-identifying tech professionals, US/UK/India)*
- **The efficiency gain, priced both ways:** 62% say AI made their workload more manageable, while 42% are expected to produce more in less time; 47% say their responsibilities became more strategic, 17% more overwhelming. Self-rated AI experts dipped to 25% from 28% in 2025 (14% in 2024), while combined advanced+expert kept rising — 35% (2024) → 65% (2025) → 70% (2026). *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, via HRKatha, Nov 11, 2026 — full-report cuts beyond the Nov 5 release)*
- **Gendered harms of AI, mapped as a cascade (women-in-tech harms only):** 20 harms identified in the snapshot; filed here are the four that support women in tech — 44% of 133 AI systems studied showed gender bias (more than a quarter both gender and racial bias); 88% of leading AI researchers globally are male; women are 30% of the global AI workforce and 30% of Australia's AI industry; 2% of venture capital went to female-only teams in 2025; and gendered digital exclusion — lower uptake of AI tools by women, a skills/AI-literacy gap, reduced participation in AI careers. *(Victorian Women's Trust, Gender and AI: Where's the Harm?, Oct 2026; bias figures via UN News, June 2026 — Australian snapshot with global figures; revised Nov 5, 2026 per Zoe's direction to women-in-tech harms only, deepfake figures removed as out of scope)*
- **AI encouragement gap, entry level (US/Canada):** only 21% of entry-level women are encouraged by their manager to use AI, vs 33% of men at the same level — and only 37% of entry-level women believe AI will improve their career prospects, vs 60% of employees overall. *(McKinsey & LeanIn.Org, Women in the Workplace 2025, Dec 2025)*
- **Europe's AI triple threat:** women fell to 19% of core tech roles (representation, not pay). Women in entry-level product development and software engineering saw the largest demand declines from 2024 to 2025 (−17% and −13%); AI, data, and analytics is the only job family with rising entry-level demand, up 11% for men and 7% for women. *(McKinsey, Women in Tech and AI in Europe, Mar 5, 2026)*
- **AI anxiety (global, all sectors):** 57% of women are concerned AI may replace their job, and 58% are concerned about their ability to learn the new skills needed for future job security. *(Deloitte, Women @ Work 2026; 7,500 women, 15 countries, fieldwork May–June 2026)*
- **AI skills (cybersecurity):** AI was the most pressing skills need cited (41% of respondents). Women participants were more likely than men to report significant knowledge of AI and machine learning (27% vs 17%), and more likely to have considered changing careers to prepare for an AI-driven future (27% vs 17%). *(ISC2, Women in Cybersecurity study, Mar 2026; 16,029 respondents — cybersecurity only)*
- **AI experimentation (Australia):** 77% of respondents say their workplace encourages them to experiment with new tools or technologies, including AI. *(Women in Digital, Driving Change in Tech 2026, Aug 2026; 1,029 respondents, Australia)*
- **Who gets the AI work:** "It isn't so much on who's getting promoted anymore. It's who's getting the AI work," says Lovelace co-author Vanessa Vallely — if women aren't given opportunities on the big AI programmes, "we will just perpetuate the bias in the systems, the processes and the things that we build." *(Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*
- **AI exposure is concentrated in women's jobs (Canada):** women hold 62.7% of employment in the 10% of occupations with the highest AI task exposure (bookkeeping, payroll administration, data entry); 64.3% of employed women aged 15–24 work in occupations where AI is far more likely to automate their core tasks entirely than to augment them. Among 55 digital occupations, women hold just 10.6% of the jobs combining high AI exposure with high potential for AI to complement human work. *(ICTC, testimony to the House of Commons Standing Committee on the Status of Women, Nov 2, 2026; research across 505 occupations)*
- **GenAI adoption among women entrepreneurs — the divide decides who gains:** 56% of the nearly 18,000 women entrepreneurs surveyed (mainly Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt) already use GenAI, mostly for writing/content (64% of users) and research (49%). Use was 65% among women able to use word-processing/spreadsheet tools independently vs 24% among those unable; 76% where family and community strongly support women's use of digital tools vs 23% where mostly not accepted; 53% of own-account workers vs 75% of women running businesses with 50+ workers. Unpaid care constrained the businesses of 59% of respondents. *(ILO, Women in Digital Business initiative, Nov 9, 2026; voluntary online survey, June–Nov 2025, not nationally representative; no income or productivity outcomes measured)*
- **A third woman takes a brand-new Chief AI Officer seat:** Barclays appointed Anita Tanna its first Chief AI Officer (in post since October 2026), after nearly three years as group chief of staff to the CEO and a career in equity sales — she has never run a technology organisation. Her brief is accelerating AI adoption across the group and finding where it returns the most value. Third brand-new CAIO seat held by a woman after Larkin (Eptura, Nov 2) and Shaver (Davis Polk, Nov 9): two rhyming press releases are now three seats in a month, across banking, worktech and law. Still appointments, not outcomes — Barclays has not said what Tanna controls, whom she reports to, or what budget she holds. *(Traders Magazine, Nov 9, 2026; FinTech Futures, Oct 26, 2026)*
- **The AI seat wave's first seat with a team (Australia):** Commonwealth Bank appointed Professor [[mary-anne-williams|Mary-Anne Williams]] its first Chief AI Scientist (announced May 18, 2026; in post June 15) — UNSW Michael J Crouch Chair for Innovation, founder and lead of the UNSW Business AI Lab, Deputy Director of the UNSW AI Institute, Stanford and AAAI fellow. She leads CommBank's team of Distinguished AI Scientists (machine learning, responsible AI, AI security, generative AI) and plays a central role in shaping the bank's AI research agenda. Unlike the three CAIO appointments filed in November, her seat comes with a team on the record from day one. *(CommBank, May 19, 2026)*
- **Agency up, visibility flat (developers):** 64% of women developers say AI improved their ability to shape their career direction and 84% report stronger career momentum linked to AI skills — but only 33% say AI adoption increased their visibility or access to opportunities. Confidence in the gender fairness of AI-supported tools (performance reviews, task allocation) runs 56% among women vs 64% among men. *(BairesDev, Dev Barometer Q1 2026; 1,329 developers across 61 countries, surveyed January 2026, 25% women — vendor survey)*
- **AI is now a pay-setting mechanism, not just a hiring one:** Fawcett warns AI systems trained on historical hiring and pay data risk reproducing pay bias across recruitment screening, shift allocation, pay setting and promotion — and may undervalue part-time working or reward visibility and hours worked over contribution. Filed as a mechanism claim, not a payroll figure. *(Fawcett Society, Nov 20, 2026)*
- **The CAIO wave reaches state government — and the seat's first holder lasted about a month:** Illinois rehired former chief data officer Tammy Roust as its second-ever chief AI officer, after Kader Sakkaria — named the inaugural CAIO in June — quietly departed after about a month in post (final day July 2). Roust brings more than 25 years of AI, data and cybersecurity leadership and most recently founded and led the AI firm FAIT Consulting. The Department of Innovation and Technology says she will "help guide Illinois' statewide strategy for the safe, responsible, and innovative use of AI." *(StateScoop, Oct 19, 2026; GovTech, Oct 20, 2026)*

## 🗣️ Real talk

**Jayeeta Putatunda**, AI engineering lead at investment firm Turing, on keeping up after time away: she took four months of maternity leave and came back to "completely different frameworks and levels of models." Catching up was overwhelming until colleagues helped and a partner shared childcare equally. "Women don't lack the interest or ability to keep up," she said — they "may lack the infrastructure that makes keeping up possible." *(TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*

**[[anjalie-field|Anjalie Field]]**, Johns Hopkins computer scientist, on whose side of the model bias research looks: most earlier work examined bias in what models produce; her prompt-register study asked how models perform for different users — and found women-associated prompt language drew weaker drafts from every chatbot tested. "If you prompt a model to write an email you're going to send to someone else at your company, and you're using language features that women more commonly use, you'll get back a response that's less complex, at a lower grade level, and less formal." *(JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*

**[[julie-chatterjee|Julie Chatterjee]]**, group CEO of Northmill Bank, on who gets to build with the new tools: she has driven AI across software development, internal operations and customer services — 2,000+ tailored welcome journeys, plus an AI-literacy push so employees across functions gain the skills to use the technology. "When organisations break down technical gatekeeping and give women across all functions the confidence to build with new technology, we naturally create a stronger pipeline into executive roles." *(FinTech Futures, Nov 5, 2026)*

**[[vanessa-vallely|Vanessa Vallely]]**, co-author of the Lovelace Report, on the question that replaced promotion: "It isn't so much on who's getting promoted anymore. It's who's getting the AI work." Her warning: if women aren't on the big AI programmes, "it's going to push us further down the stack." *(Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*

**[[mary-anne-williams|Mary-Anne Williams]]**, on why she took the bank's first Chief AI Scientist role: "My focus will be on advancing our understanding of the societal implications of AI and supporting continued responsible AI innovation across the bank." *(CommBank, May 19, 2026)*

## 💔 Why it matters

If AI eats the entry-level work where people learn, the first rung of the ladder gets thinner, and women already lose ground at the rungs above. If women build fewer of the tools, the tools are less likely to see them.

## 💭 Still brewing

- Does AI automation hit roles women hold more often, like support, coordination, and annotation, harder than the roles men hold? ICTC's Canada numbers (Nov 13) give a partial answer: yes on exposure — 62.7% of employment in the highest-exposure occupations is women's, and 64.3% of young women sit in automate-rather-than-augment roles. The open part is what happens next: augmentation, displacement, or neither.
- AI in hiring and promotion: does it reduce the bias at 3.5, or bake it in?
- Who gets the AI upskilling and the high-visibility AI projects? That's the [[exposure-gap]] all over again, unless someone sponsors it ([[sponsorship-gap]]). Partial answer, Nov 14: Shorenstein says women get less training, mentorship and encouragement — and that support tracks use and confidence for both genders. The Danish study says the same from the other side: women non-users cite a need for training, men cite employer restrictions. So the upskilling gap is a support gap, and the fix is named — but nobody has shown it moving women into the senior roles yet.
- Ireland is the counterpoint to the US pattern: the gender gap in GenAI use is small (29.0% men vs 27.8% women) and doesn't widen with intensity — unlike Pew and Shorenstein. Is that composition (which occupations women hold), policy, or measurement (intentional use vs ever-use)? One dataset can't settle it, but the US pattern is no longer the pattern.
- Where does this attach to the tree? It started at 3.1, but it may touch 2.3 (the jump to leadership) and 3.4 (resource work) too.
- The $45,000 AI pay figure is a composition effect (a 10-point rise in the male share of hires was associated with ~$45,000 higher median listed pay across AI occupations), not a like-for-like gap. What *is* the like-for-like AI pay gap? We don't have that number yet.
- Nov 2026 evidence speaks to the 3.5 question above: the JHU prompt study says the bias is baked in before any hiring decision, in the drafting tool itself. Open: do users adapt their style over time to match what AI rewards (the authors' next question)?
- The CAIO seats: two women in brand-new seats in a week (Larkin, Shaver) was a data point, not a trend — now Tanna at Barclays makes three in a month, across banking, worktech and law. Is seat-creation a wave? The test is the next quarter's appointments, and whether any of the three gets a budget, a team, or a reporting line on the record — and whether the 13% of C-suite AI roles receipt moves. Update, Nov 18: Williams at CBA is the first seat in the wave with a team on the record — she leads CommBank's Distinguished AI Scientists — though she is a Chief AI Scientist, not a CAIO, and her appointment (May/June) predates the Larkin/Shaver/Tanna wave. The test for the three CAIOs still stands.
