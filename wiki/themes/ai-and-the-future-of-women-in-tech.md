# 🤖 AI and the future of women in tech

## ☕ The tea

Girl, every new wave of tech is a fresh chance to get it right, and the early receipts say we're repeating the old pattern. When AI roles open up, women land in the lower-paid ones. It grew out of node 3.1 on the original tree, and it's a theme of its own because AI is reshaping entry-level work, promotion paths, and who gets to build the tools.

## 🧾 Receipts

- **3.1, who gets which AI job:** women hold 45% of data annotator roles and 19% of AI engineer roles. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- Data annotators earn less than the median non-AI job. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- **Hiring flow, 2025:** women were 26% of new AI hires in the US vs 50% of new non-AI hires, and held 13% of C-suite AI roles across 27 countries. *(LinkedIn research, via TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*
- **Perceived impact:** 31% of women said AI had improved gender equality, 19% said it had made it worse, 34% saw no impact, 16% unsure. *(Web Summit Gender in Tech 2026, 1,000+ women in Web Summit's community — not a representative sample of the whole workforce)*
- **Prompt register:** prompts carrying women-associated linguistic features (hedges, tag questions, collective reference, expressive adjectives) elicited shorter, less sophisticated, less formal responses across three document types and four models (GPT-4, Llama, Gemma, Mistral); effects persisted after controlling for prompt complexity and style carry-over, and a male sign-off name had virtually no effect. *(Van Koevering & Field, Johns Hopkins, "It's How You Ask: Gender-Associated Linguistic Bias in LLMs", COLM 2026; JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*
- **LLM-as-judge, preliminary:** in a partial GPT-4-only judge run (105 comparisons), the male-associated prompt output was chosen as more competent in 69.4% of decided comparisons and smarter in 68.3%; the judge tied most of the time (67 ties on competence). The authors call the analysis partial and single-sample. *(same paper, Table 7)*

## 🗣️ Real talk

**Jayeeta Putatunda**, AI engineering lead at investment firm Turing, on keeping up after time away: she took four months of maternity leave and came back to "completely different frameworks and levels of models." Catching up was overwhelming until colleagues helped and a partner shared childcare equally. "Women don't lack the interest or ability to keep up," she said — they "may lack the infrastructure that makes keeping up possible." *(TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*

## 💔 Why it matters

If AI eats the entry-level work where people learn, the first rung of the ladder gets thinner, and women already lose ground at the rungs above. If women build fewer of the tools, the tools are less likely to see them.

## 💭 Still brewing

- Does AI automation hit roles women hold more often, like support, coordination, and annotation, harder than the roles men hold?
- AI in hiring and promotion: does it reduce the bias at 3.5, or bake it in?
- Who gets the AI upskilling and the high-visibility AI projects? That's the [[exposure-gap]] all over again, unless someone sponsors it ([[sponsorship-gap]]).
- Where does this attach to the tree? It started at 3.1, but it may touch 2.3 (the jump to leadership) and 3.4 (resource work) too.
- The $45,000 AI pay figure is a composition effect (a 10-point rise in the male share of hires was associated with ~$45,000 higher median listed pay across AI occupations), not a like-for-like gap. What *is* the like-for-like AI pay gap? We don't have that number yet.
- Nov 2026 evidence speaks to the 3.5 question above: the JHU prompt study says the bias is baked in before any hiring decision, in the drafting tool itself. Open: do users adapt their style over time to match what AI rewards (the authors' next question)?
