# 🤖 AI and the future of women in tech

## ☕ The tea

Girl, every new wave of tech is a fresh chance to get it right, and the early receipts say we're repeating the old pattern. When AI roles open up, women land in the lower-paid ones. It grew out of node 3.1 on the original tree, and it's a theme of its own because AI is reshaping entry-level work, promotion paths, and who gets to build the tools.

## 🧾 Receipts

- **3.1, who gets which AI job:** women hold 45% of data annotator roles and 19% of AI engineer roles. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- Data annotators earn less than the median non-AI job. *(WEF 2026, Figure 2.10)*
- **Hiring flow, 2025:** women were 26% of new AI hires in the US vs 50% of new non-AI hires, and held 13% of C-suite AI roles across 27 countries. *(LinkedIn research, via TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*
- **Perceived impact:** 31% of women said AI had improved gender equality, 19% said it had made it worse, 34% saw no impact, 16% unsure. *(Web Summit Gender in Tech 2026, 1,000+ women in Web Summit's community — not a representative sample of the whole workforce)*
- **Prompt register:** prompts carrying women-associated linguistic features (hedges, tag questions, collective reference, expressive adjectives) elicited shorter, less sophisticated, less formal responses across three document types and four models (GPT-4, Llama, Gemma, Mistral); effects persisted after controlling for prompt complexity and style carry-over, and a male sign-off name had virtually no effect. *(Van Koevering & Field, Johns Hopkins, "It's How You Ask: Gender-Associated Linguistic Bias in LLMs", COLM 2026; JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*
- **LLM-as-judge, preliminary:** in a partial GPT-4-only judge run (105 comparisons), the male-associated prompt output was chosen as more competent in 69.4% of decided comparisons and smarter in 68.3%; the judge tied most of the time (67 ties on competence). The authors call the analysis partial and single-sample. *(same paper, Table 7)*
- **Automation risk, uneven:** 21% of women's employment is in high-automation-risk occupations vs 10% of men's; 9% of women's jobs vs 13% of men's are projected to grow with AI. Scenario analysis grounded in the Richmond AI Jobs Transition Framework, not predictions; the authors caution the framework may overstate substitution risk. *(Philadelphia Fed, "Which Workers Are Most Vulnerable to AI Disruption?", circulated Oct 30, 2026; BLS OEWS + ACS PUMS data)*
- **AI skills up, workload up:** 70% of women in tech rate their generative AI knowledge and skills as advanced or expert (experts doubled since 2024), yet 42% say they are expected to produce more work in less time because of AI; 87% report at least some level of burnout, 60% moderate, very or extreme. *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, Nov 5, 2026; 1,500 female-identifying tech professionals, US/UK/India)*
- **The efficiency gain, priced both ways:** 62% say AI made their workload more manageable, while 42% are expected to produce more in less time; 47% say their responsibilities became more strategic, 17% more overwhelming. Self-rated AI experts dipped to 25% from 28% in 2025 (14% in 2024), while combined advanced+expert kept rising — 35% (2024) → 65% (2025) → 70% (2026). *(Ensono, Speak Up 2026, via HRKatha, Nov 11, 2026 — full-report cuts beyond the Nov 5 release)*
- **Gendered harms of AI, mapped as a cascade (women-in-tech harms only):** 20 harms identified in the snapshot; filed here are the four that support women in tech — 44% of 133 AI systems studied showed gender bias (more than a quarter both gender and racial bias); 88% of leading AI researchers globally are male; women are 30% of the global AI workforce and 30% of Australia's AI industry; 2% of venture capital went to female-only teams in 2025; and gendered digital exclusion — lower uptake of AI tools by women, a skills/AI-literacy gap, reduced participation in AI careers. *(Victorian Women's Trust, Gender and AI: Where's the Harm?, Oct 2026; bias figures via UN News, June 2026 — Australian snapshot with global figures; revised Nov 5, 2026 per Zoe's direction to women-in-tech harms only, deepfake figures removed as out of scope)*
- **AI encouragement gap, entry level (US/Canada):** only 21% of entry-level women are encouraged by their manager to use AI, vs 33% of men at the same level — and only 37% of entry-level women believe AI will improve their career prospects, vs 60% of employees overall. *(McKinsey & LeanIn.Org, Women in the Workplace 2025, Dec 2025)*
- **Europe's AI triple threat:** women fell to 19% of core tech roles (representation, not pay). Women in entry-level product development and software engineering saw the largest demand declines from 2024 to 2025 (−17% and −13%); AI, data, and analytics is the only job family with rising entry-level demand, up 11% for men and 7% for women. *(McKinsey, Women in Tech and AI in Europe, Mar 5, 2026)*
- **AI anxiety (global, all sectors):** 57% of women are concerned AI may replace their job, and 58% are concerned about their ability to learn the new skills needed for future job security. *(Deloitte, Women @ Work 2026; 7,500 women, 15 countries, fieldwork May–June 2026)*
- **AI skills (cybersecurity):** AI was the most pressing skills need cited (41% of respondents). Women participants were more likely than men to report significant knowledge of AI and machine learning (27% vs 17%), and more likely to have considered changing careers to prepare for an AI-driven future (27% vs 17%). *(ISC2, Women in Cybersecurity study, Mar 2026; 16,029 respondents — cybersecurity only)*
- **AI experimentation (Australia):** 77% of respondents say their workplace encourages them to experiment with new tools or technologies, including AI. *(Women in Digital, Driving Change in Tech 2026, Aug 2026; 1,029 respondents, Australia)*
- **Who gets the AI work:** "It isn't so much on who's getting promoted anymore. It's who's getting the AI work," says Lovelace co-author Vanessa Vallely — if women aren't given opportunities on the big AI programmes, "we will just perpetuate the bias in the systems, the processes and the things that we build." *(Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*

## 🗣️ Real talk

**Jayeeta Putatunda**, AI engineering lead at investment firm Turing, on keeping up after time away: she took four months of maternity leave and came back to "completely different frameworks and levels of models." Catching up was overwhelming until colleagues helped and a partner shared childcare equally. "Women don't lack the interest or ability to keep up," she said — they "may lack the infrastructure that makes keeping up possible." *(TechTimes, Oct 5, 2026)*

**[[anjalie-field|Anjalie Field]]**, Johns Hopkins computer scientist, on whose side of the model bias research looks: most earlier work examined bias in what models produce; her prompt-register study asked how models perform for different users — and found women-associated prompt language drew weaker drafts from every chatbot tested. "If you prompt a model to write an email you're going to send to someone else at your company, and you're using language features that women more commonly use, you'll get back a response that's less complex, at a lower grade level, and less formal." *(JHU Hub, Sept 21, 2026)*

**[[julie-chatterjee|Julie Chatterjee]]**, group CEO of Northmill Bank, on who gets to build with the new tools: she has driven AI across software development, internal operations and customer services — 2,000+ tailored welcome journeys, plus an AI-literacy push so employees across functions gain the skills to use the technology. "When organisations break down technical gatekeeping and give women across all functions the confidence to build with new technology, we naturally create a stronger pipeline into executive roles." *(FinTech Futures, Nov 5, 2026)*

**[[vanessa-vallely|Vanessa Vallely]]**, co-author of the Lovelace Report, on the question that replaced promotion: "It isn't so much on who's getting promoted anymore. It's who's getting the AI work." Her warning: if women aren't on the big AI programmes, "it's going to push us further down the stack." *(Prolific North, Nov 4, 2026)*

## 💔 Why it matters

If AI eats the entry-level work where people learn, the first rung of the ladder gets thinner, and women already lose ground at the rungs above. If women build fewer of the tools, the tools are less likely to see them.

## 💭 Still brewing

- Does AI automation hit roles women hold more often, like support, coordination, and annotation, harder than the roles men hold?
- AI in hiring and promotion: does it reduce the bias at 3.5, or bake it in?
- Who gets the AI upskilling and the high-visibility AI projects? That's the [[exposure-gap]] all over again, unless someone sponsors it ([[sponsorship-gap]]).
- Where does this attach to the tree? It started at 3.1, but it may touch 2.3 (the jump to leadership) and 3.4 (resource work) too.
- The $45,000 AI pay figure is a composition effect (a 10-point rise in the male share of hires was associated with ~$45,000 higher median listed pay across AI occupations), not a like-for-like gap. What *is* the like-for-like AI pay gap? We don't have that number yet.
- Nov 2026 evidence speaks to the 3.5 question above: the JHU prompt study says the bias is baked in before any hiring decision, in the drafting tool itself. Open: do users adapt their style over time to match what AI rewards (the authors' next question)?
